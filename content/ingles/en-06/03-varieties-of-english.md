# Varieties of English: US, UK, Australian, Indian, World Englishes and register shifts

Proficiency means operating comfortably across the varieties of English you will actually meet: a British auditor, an American client, an Indian outsourcing team, an Australian supplier, and the international English of a conference call with Germans and Koreans. This lesson maps the main differences in lexis, grammar, spelling and pronunciation, introduces the concept of World Englishes, and then turns to register shifting: moving between slang and professional speech, and knowing which variety and register your document should follow.

> **Glossário de apoio:** *variety* = variedade (dialeto nacional); *World Englishes* = os ingleses do mundo (variedades legítimas, não "erros"); *register shift* = mudança de registro; *slang* = gíria; *code-switching* = alternância de código; *lingua franca* = língua de contato comum.

Indian English adds its own lexis: *prepone* (bring forward: the logical opposite of postpone), *lakh* and *crore* (100,000 and 10 million, used in financial documents), *do the needful* (do what is necessary), *revert* (reply: *please revert by Friday*), *out of station* (away from one's town), *pass out* (graduate). These are standard in Indian professional English, not errors.

## 2. Grammar: small but systematic differences

| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| Present perfect vs past simple | *I already ate.* / *Did you finish yet?* | *I've already eaten.* / *Have you finished yet?* |
| Collective nouns | *The board has decided.* (singular) | *The board have decided.* (plural possible) |
| *have got* | less common: *Do you have…?* | *Have you got…?* |
| *gotten* | past participle *gotten* exists | *got* only |
| Prepositions | *on the weekend, Monday through Friday, different than, write me* | *at the weekend, Monday to Friday, different from/to, write to me* |
| Dates | 10/16/2026 | 16/10/2026 |
| Subjunctive | *It is essential that he be informed.* (common) | *…that he should be / is informed.* (more common) |
| *shall* | rare | still used in offers and legal texts |
| Past forms | *learned, burned, spelled* | *learnt, burnt, spelt* also possible |

Australian grammar follows UK, with more informal features in speech (*youse* as a plural *you* in some speakers, *good on ya*). Indian English frequently uses the progressive with stative verbs (*I am having two brothers*), question tags *isn't it? / no?* for everything, and *only* for emphasis (*I was there only*). In international business, these are intelligible and should be understood without judgment.

The rule for your documents: **pick one variety and be consistent**. A report that writes *organisation* in paragraph one and *organization* in paragraph three looks careless. Set your spell-checker accordingly. IELTS accepts either; CPE accepts either but penalizes mixing.

## 4. Pronunciation: the headline differences

- **Rhoticity:** General American, Canadian, Scottish and Irish English pronounce /r/ wherever it is written (*car* = /kɑːr/); RP, Australian, New Zealand and most accents of England drop it before consonants and at the end of a word (*car* = /kɑː/). Standard Indian English is generally non-rhotic too, although many speakers vary.
- **/t/ between vowels:** US flaps it (*better* → "bedder"); UK keeps /t/ or uses a glottal stop in informal speech.
- **Vowels:** *bath, glass, dance* have /ɑː/ in RP and southern England but /æ/ in the US and northern England; Australian *day, mate* approach "die, mite" to foreign ears; US *hot* is /hɑːt/, UK /hɒt/.
- **Stress:** *ad-VER-tis-ment* (UK) vs *ad-ver-TISE-ment* (US); *la-BOR-a-tory* (UK) vs *LAB-ra-tory* (US); *con-TRO-ver-sy* (UK, alongside *CON-tro-ver-sy*) vs *CON-tro-ver-sy* (US).
- **Indian English:** retroflex /t/ and /d/, syllable-timed rhythm (closer to Portuguese), /v/ and /w/ merged.
- **Intonation:** Australian and some US speakers use "uptalk" (rising intonation on statements); do not read it as uncertainty.

## 5. World Englishes and English as a lingua franca

English today has more non-native than native speakers. Linguists recognize three circles: the **inner circle** (UK, US, Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand), the **outer circle** where English is an official second language with its own standards (India, Nigeria, Singapore, the Philippines), and the **expanding circle** where it is a foreign language (Brazil, China, Germany). Outer-circle varieties are legitimate standards, not deficient versions of British English.

Practical consequences for you:

- In mixed international meetings, the most intelligible English is often **not** the native speaker's: slower, fewer idioms, clearer stress. Native speakers who do not adapt are the biggest comprehension problem in global teams.
- Avoid culturally loaded idioms (*sticky wicket*) and phrasal verbs with multiple meanings when writing for an international audience; prefer *postpone* to *put off*.
- Accommodate: mirror your counterpart's variety for spelling and dates in documents, and for key terms (*debtors* vs *receivables*) in conversation.
- {{en:When in doubt, choose clarity over authenticity.}}

## 6. Register shifts: slang vs professional speech

A proficient speaker **code-switches** fluently. The same idea at four levels:

| Register | Example |
|---|---|
| Slang / very informal | *The numbers are totally off. Someone screwed up big time.* |
| Casual professional (colleagues) | *The figures don't add up. Looks like something went wrong in the reconciliation.* |
| Neutral professional (client email) | *There appears to be a discrepancy in the figures, which we are investigating.* |
| Formal written (audit report) | *A material discrepancy was identified, the cause of which is under review.* |

Current informal and slang items you should recognize but use with care: *a no-brainer* (obvious decision), *to ping someone* (send a quick message), *bandwidth* (capacity: *I don't have the bandwidth*), *to circle back*, *to touch base*, *low-hanging fruit*, *to take something offline* (discuss later), *deep dive*, *to level with someone* (be honest), *to be on the same page*, *to flag* (point out), *a heads-up*, *ASAP*, *EOD/COB* (end of day / close of business), *to loop someone in*, *FYI*, *to be swamped*, *to crunch the numbers*.

{{en:Just a heads-up: I'll loop you in on the thread once I've crunched the numbers.}}

Corporate buzzwords (*synergy, leverage* as a verb, *going forward, paradigm shift, think outside the box*) are widely mocked; use them knowingly, if at all.

## Reading: critical text

The following is an email exchange within an international team. Identify the varieties and registers, and consider what could go wrong.

> From: Priya (Mumbai). Dear Tom, As discussed, kindly do the needful regarding the Q3 reconciliation and revert by Thursday. The client has preponed the review to the 20th, so we need to close the open items at the earliest. Total exposure is around 2.4 crore, which I have converted in the attached sheet. Thanks and regards, Priya.
>
> From: Tom (Sydney). Hi Priya, no worries, I'll get onto it this arvo and ping you tomorrow. Quick heads-up: the debtors ledger's a bit of a mess, so the recon might take longer than we reckoned. Cheers, Tom.
>
> From: Hannah (Chicago). Hey both, just looping in Dave from the client side. Dave, we're gonna need your receivables aging by COB Wednesday so Tom has bandwidth to turn this around. Appreciate it! Hannah.
>
> From: Dave (London). Dear all, Thank you. I should point out that the review date has not, as far as I am aware, been formally confirmed. I shall seek clarification and revert. Kind regards, David Hargreaves.

Questions: Which writer uses the most formal register, and how might the others read it? List three variety-specific words. Why might "revert" confuse a British reader, and why does Dave use it anyway? What does "a bit of a mess" probably mean?

## Na prática

An accounting office with foreign clients meets several Englishes in a single week: an American client's *income statement*, a British auditor's *P&L* and *turnover*, an Indian team's *do the needful*. Pick the client's variety and keep it consistent across the engagement letter, the spreadsheets and the e-mails: {{en:Please find the inventory figures and the revised income statement attached.}} In international calls, clarity beats idiom: speak slowly, avoid phrasal-verb chains and confirm numbers in writing. Reserve office jargon for colleagues: {{en:Can you loop me in on the payroll thread?}} is fine internally, never in a letter to a regulator.

## Resumo

- US and UK English differ systematically in lexis (*inventory / stock, income statement / P&L*), grammar (present perfect, collective nouns, prepositions, dates) and spelling (*-ize/-ise, -or/-our, -er/-re*); Australia follows UK with its own informal lexis; Indian English has legitimate standards (*prepone, revert, lakh, crore*).
- Pronunciation differences center on rhoticity, /t/ flapping, the *bath* vowel and word stress; uptalk is not uncertainty.
- World Englishes are standards, not errors; in international settings, clarity beats native-like idiom, and native speakers who do not adapt are the main comprehension problem.
- Pick one variety per document and be consistent; mirror your counterpart's conventions in correspondence.
- Code-switch across registers deliberately: slang and office jargon (*heads-up, loop in, bandwidth*) with colleagues; neutral or formal forms in anything a client or regulator may read.
