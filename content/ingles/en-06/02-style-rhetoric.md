# Style and rhetoric: persuasion, tone, irony and wordplay

This lesson is about effect. You will study the classical persuasive devices that still structure speeches, editorials and marketing (tricolon, anaphora, antithesis, rhetorical questions), learn to control tone across a text, decode and produce irony and sarcasm, understand how English humor and wordplay work, and recognize the cultural references that native speakers drop without explanation. At C2 these are not ornaments: they are how educated speakers signal wit, authority and belonging.

> **Glossário de apoio:** *tricolon* = tríade, série de três; *anaphora* = anáfora (repetição no início de frases); *antithesis* = antítese; *rhetorical question* = pergunta retórica; *tone* = tom; *irony* = ironia; *sarcasm* = sarcasmo (ironia agressiva); *pun* = trocadilho; *deadpan* = sem expressão, sério (humor).

## 1. Persuasive devices

**Tricolon** (three parallel elements): English ears find three the most satisfying number. {{en:We will cut costs, raise standards and restore trust.}} The third element is often the longest or most important (*ascending tricolon*): *Friends, Romans, countrymen*. Business variant: *faster, cheaper and far more reliable*.

**Anaphora** (repetition at the start of successive clauses): {{en:We did not ask for this crisis. We did not create this crisis. But we will end it.}} Churchill's *We shall fight on the beaches…* is the classic. In reports, a muted form: *First, … Second, … Third, …*.

**Antithesis** (balanced opposites): {{en:Ask not what your country can do for you; ask what you can do for your country.}} Business: *It is not a question of whether we change but of how.* / *We hire for attitude and train for skill.*

**Rhetorical question**: a question that asserts. {{en:Can we really afford another year of indecision?}} (= we cannot). Use sparingly in formal writing; one per text is powerful, three is preaching.

**Other devices:** *epistrophe* (repetition at the end: *…of the people, by the people, for the people*), *chiasmus* (ABBA structure: *Work to live, don't live to work*), *climax* (ascending order), *alliteration* (*profit, people, planet*), *parallelism* (matching grammatical structure, which also prevents the errors covered in Lesson 7).

## 2. Tone control

Tone is the writer's attitude toward the subject and the reader. It must be **consistent** within a text and **appropriate** to the genre. The same fact in four tones:

| Tone | Sentence |
|---|---|
| Neutral/informative | *Profits fell by 12% in the third quarter.* |
| Reassuring | *Although profits dipped in the third quarter, the underlying business remains robust.* |
| Alarmed | *A 12% collapse in quarterly profits should set alarm bells ringing.* |
| Ironic | *The third quarter brought a 12% drop in profits, which management has described, with admirable creativity, as a "rebasing".* |

Tone is created by lexis (*dipped* vs *collapse*), by modality (*may* vs *will*), by sentence length (short = urgent), by punctuation (dashes = informal energy) and by the presence or absence of evaluation. A classic C2 error is **tonal drift**: starting formal and sliding into chatty, or inserting one sarcastic sentence into an otherwise neutral report. Decide the tone before writing and audit for it afterward.

## 3. Irony and sarcasm

**Irony** says one thing and means another, relying on shared knowledge. **Verbal irony**: {{en:What a wonderful time for the server to crash.}} **Situational irony**: the fire station burned down. **Dramatic irony** (in fiction): the audience knows what the character does not.

**Sarcasm** is irony with a target and an edge; it is meant to wound or mock. *Oh, brilliant. Another meeting about meetings.* British speech uses irony constantly and often deadpan (without any signal in the voice), which is why Brazilians sometimes take it literally. Markers to listen for: exaggerated praise for something trivial (*Genius.*), flat intonation, *Oh, …*, *How very…*, *I'm sure…* (*I'm sure the regulator will be delighted*).

In writing, irony is signaled by scare quotes ("rebasing"), by *so-called*, by *apparently*, by *of course* in a context where nothing is obvious, and by the juxtaposition of grand language with trivial content. {{en:The committee, after eighteen months of intensive deliberation, has decided to change the font.}}

Professional rule: irony is risky across cultures and in writing that may be forwarded. Use it with people who know you; never in client-facing documents.

## 4. Humor and wordplay

English humor relies heavily on language itself.

- **Puns** (same or similar sound, two meanings): *The accountant's favorite film? The Sum of All Fears.* / *A boiled egg every morning is hard to beat.* Headlines live on puns: *Bank's profits take interest.*
- **Double meaning in idioms**: *The balance sheet is looking a bit unbalanced.*
- **Understatement as humor** (Lesson 1): *He's not the sharpest tool in the shed.* (UK: *in the box*)
- **Self-deprecation**: the British default: *I'm no expert, but…* (often said by the expert). Brazilians who praise themselves directly can sound arrogant to British ears; Americans are more tolerant of confident self-presentation.
- **Absurdist and surreal humor** (Monty Python tradition): logic pushed to extremes.
- **Bathos**: a sudden drop from the elevated to the trivial: *He fought for justice, for freedom, and for a better parking space.*

Recognizing wordplay is a C2 reading skill (CPE texts often contain it); producing it is optional but socially valuable in meetings and small talk.

## 5. Cultural references

Native speakers allude without explaining. Core references you should recognize:

- **Literary/Biblical:** *a Catch-22* (no-win situation), *Big Brother* (surveillance), *Orwellian*, *Kafkaesque*, *a Scrooge* (miser), *a Pyrrhic victory*, *the writing on the wall* (imminent doom), *a good Samaritan*, *a David and Goliath contest*, *a Faustian bargain*, *Hobson's choice* (no real choice), *the road to hell is paved with good intentions*.
- **Historical/political:** *crossing the Rubicon*, *meeting one's Waterloo*, *a Marshall Plan for…*, *Watergate* (hence the suffix *-gate* for any scandal), *the Iron Curtain*, *a Trojan horse*.
- **Sport:** *a sticky wicket* (UK, awkward situation), *a curveball* (US, surprise), *the whole nine yards* (US), *step up to the plate*, *moving the goalposts*.
- **Film/TV:** *Groundhog Day* (endless repetition), *a Jekyll and Hyde character* (from Stevenson's novella, now mostly known through film), *Houston, we have a problem*, *jump the shark* (decline in quality).

{{en:Negotiating with that supplier is Groundhog Day: every meeting, the same objections.}}

## 6. Rhetoric in business documents

Executives and consultants use rhetoric constantly but discreetly. In an executive summary: one tricolon in the opening ("three priorities"), parallel headings, antithesis in the recommendation (*not cost-cutting but cost-shifting*), a single rhetorical question at most. In a pitch: anaphora for momentum, a cultural reference for warmth, a closing climax. In a complaint: controlled irony is sometimes effective (*We were, of course, delighted to receive the third incorrect invoice*), but only when the relationship is secure.

## 7. Typical errors made by Portuguese speakers

| Erro | Correção | Comentário |
|---|---|---|
| Levar ironia britânica ao pé da letra | Ouvir a entonação plana e o exagero | "Brilliant" dito sem emoção = problema. |
| Perguntas retóricas em série em ensaio | Uma, no máximo | Soa como sermão. |
| Tricolon desbalanceado: ~~fast, cheap and it is reliable~~ | fast, cheap and reliable | Paralelismo gramatical. |
| Autoelogio direto em contexto britânico | Autodepreciação leve | Diferença cultural. |
| Sarcasmo em e-mail para cliente | Tom neutro | Pode ser encaminhado e lido sem contexto. |
| ~~a Catch-22 situation where you can choose~~ | a Catch-22 (no choice works) | Referência mal aplicada. |
| Mudança de tom no meio do texto | Definir o tom e revisar | Tonal drift. |
| Traduzir trocadilhos | Não traduzir; explicar ou substituir | Jogo de palavras não sobrevive à tradução. |

## Reading: critical text

The extract below is from a newspaper editorial. Identify the rhetorical devices, the tone and the irony.

> For the third year running, the regulator has published its annual review, and for the third year running it has discovered, to its evident surprise, that the firms it regulates are not behaving. The report runs to 212 pages. It contains seventeen recommendations, nine "areas of focus" and one conclusion, which is that more study is needed. We have been here before. We were here in 2023, when the review promised "decisive action". We were here in 2024, when it promised "a step change". We are here again, and the step has not changed. Is it unreasonable to ask what, precisely, the regulator is for? The firms, naturally, welcome the report. They would. A watchdog that barks on schedule and never bites is the best friend a bad actor could wish for. The public, meanwhile, pays twice: once for the misconduct and once for the review that describes it. Reform the regulator, abolish it, or stop pretending. Any of the three would be an improvement on the present arrangement, which is a very expensive way of writing the same report.

Questions: Find the anaphora, the tricolon and the rhetorical question. What is ironic about "to its evident surprise"? What does the two-word sentence "They would." imply? Is the tone angry, contemptuous or weary, and which words tell you?

## Na prática

Rhetoric has a narrow but real place in an accountant's work: the opening of a presentation, the conclusion of a proposal, a note that must persuade a reluctant client to act. A tricolon frames the recommendation memorably: {{en:We need accurate records, timely filings and a reserve that reflects reality.}} A controlled rhetorical question invites agreement: {{en:Can a company plan its growth without knowing its true margin?}} Keep irony out of client communication entirely; a deadpan remark in an e-mail reads as contempt. Decide the tone before writing, use one device per document and let the figures do the rest.

## Resumo

- Tricolon, anaphora, antithesis and the rhetorical question structure persuasive English from Churchill to board presentations; parallelism is their grammatical backbone.
- Tone is created by lexis, modality, sentence length and evaluation; decide it before writing and audit for tonal drift.
- Irony says one thing and means another; sarcasm adds a target. British speech is often deadpan; decode exaggerated praise, flat intonation and *of course / so-called* in writing. Avoid irony in client-facing documents.
- English humor runs on puns, understatement, self-deprecation and bathos; recognizing wordplay is a C2 reading skill.
- Cultural references (*Catch-22, Orwellian, cross the Rubicon, Groundhog Day, -gate*) are used without explanation; learn the core set.
