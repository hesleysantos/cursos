# Capstone: analytical report, ten-minute talk, critical review, and a plan for life at C2

This final lesson has three parts: the **capstone project** (an 800-word analytical report, a self-recorded ten-minute talk and a critical review of a complex text, each with a rubric and a model), guidance on honest **self-assessment**, and a **maintenance plan**, because proficiency decays without use.

> **Glossário de apoio:** *capstone* = projeto final integrador; *rubric* = grade de critérios; *band descriptor* = descritor de faixa; *self-assessment* = autoavaliação; *to plateau* = estagnar; *attrition* = perda gradual (de competência); *immersion* = imersão; *spaced repetition* = repetição espaçada.

## Part 1: The capstone project

### Task A: Analytical report (800 words)

**Brief.** Choose an issue you know professionally (mandatory e-invoicing for small firms; whether Brazil's tax reform will simplify compliance; a four-day week in professional services; AI and junior accounting roles). Write an 800-word analytical report for a named reader (a managing partner, a client's board, a professional body) that defines the issue and scope, presents at least two perspectives, analyzes causes and consequences, evaluates options and makes a justified recommendation.

**Required structure.** Title; executive summary (80–100 words, Module 6 Lesson 5); numbered sections with headings (Introduction and scope / Background / Analysis / Options / Recommendation); a short conclusion. Use at least: two complex noun phrases, one mandative subjunctive, one inversion or cleft, one participle opening, one semicolon and one colon used for effect, two precision verbs, and one hedged claim. No contractions, no slang, no comma splices.

**Rubric (score each criterion 1–5):**

| Criterion | 5 (C2) | 3 (C1) | 1 (B2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Content and analysis | Issue precisely defined; multiple perspectives weighed; causes and consequences distinguished; recommendation follows from analysis | Issue defined; two perspectives listed; recommendation plausible but loosely linked | Description rather than analysis; one perspective; recommendation asserted |
| Organization | Executive summary stands alone; headings guide; thematic progression within paragraphs; referencing varied | Structure present; some paragraphs lack topic sentences; connectors overused | Weak paragraphing; ideas in order of occurrence |
| Register and tone | Consistently formal and impersonal; tone controlled; no drift | Mostly formal; one or two slips | Mixed register; personal voice intrudes |
| Lexical range and precision | Precision verbs; near-synonyms chosen by nuance; finance register exact; collocations natural | Good range; occasional imprecision; some generic verbs | Repetitive; generic vocabulary; false friends |
| Grammatical range and accuracy | Subjunctive, inversion, cleft, participles, complex NPs used naturally; errors almost absent | Range present but effortful; a few errors | Limited range; recurrent errors |
| Concision and editing | Every sentence informative; no redundancy; punctuation purposeful | Some wordiness; punctuation mostly correct | Wordy; comma splices; misplaced modifiers |

Passing standard for C2: a total of 25 or more out of 30, with no criterion below 4.

**Model opening** (executive summary and first paragraph):

> Executive summary. This report assesses the likely impact of mandatory electronic invoicing on firms with fewer than twenty employees in the services sector. It finds that compliance costs are front-loaded and regressive, falling hardest on the smallest firms, but that these costs are largely recouped within eighteen months through faster collection and reduced disputes. It recommends that the professional body lobby for a twelve-month phased introduction and a subsidized software scheme, and that member firms be advised to adopt early rather than wait for the deadline.
>
> 1. Introduction and scope. Few regulatory changes of the past decade have provoked as much anxiety among small practitioners as the move to mandatory e-invoicing. That anxiety is not groundless: the evidence reviewed below indicates that transition costs are real and unevenly distributed. What the public debate has largely overlooked, however, is the asymmetry between those costs, which are temporary, and the benefits, which recur. This report therefore examines both sides of the ledger. It draws on published impact assessments from three jurisdictions, on survey data from 140 member firms and on interviews with eight practitioners who have already made the transition.

### Task B: Ten-minute talk (self-recorded)

**Brief.** Record ten minutes on the topic of your report for colleagues who have not read it. No script; at most ten bullet points. Then listen back and score yourself.

**Structure.** Hook and purpose (1 min); roadmap (30 s); three signposted sections with an example each (2 min each); two anticipated objections (1 min); recommendation and close (1 min); then answer aloud three questions a skeptical colleague might ask.

**Rubric (1–5 each):**

| Criterion | What a 5 looks like |
|---|---|
| Structure and signposting | Listener always knows where they are; transitions explicit; summary and close |
| Fluency and coherence | Sustained speech with natural pausing; self-correction smooth; no long silences |
| Pronunciation and prosody | Stress-timed rhythm; weak forms; nuclear stress on new information; intonation conveys attitude (fall for certainty, fall-rise for reservation) |
| Lexical and grammatical range | Spoken register with idiom and precision; complex structures used without hesitation |
| Interaction and Q&A | Objections anticipated; questions reframed and answered with structure; hostile question handled gracefully |
| Length and discipline | Ten minutes ±30 s; no new material in the close |

**Self-listening protocol.** Listen once for structure, once for language (three errors, three good phrases), once for delivery (where did rhythm become syllable-timed?). Re-record the weakest two minutes.

{{en:That brings me to the second objection, which is that early adopters bear all the risk and none of the reward.}}

### Task C: Critical review of a complex text

**Brief.** Choose a demanding text of 1,500–3,000 words: a long-form editorial from *The Economist*, *The Atlantic* or the *Financial Times*; a chapter of a public-domain novel; or an academic paper's introduction and conclusion. Write a 400-word critical review that: summarizes the argument in your own words (Lesson 6); identifies the author's stance and the devices that create it (Lessons 1–2); evaluates the evidence and reasoning; and states your own assessment with justification.

**Rubric highlights.** Faithful, attributed summary; stance identified from textual evidence (at most two short quotations); claim, evidence and assumption distinguished; your assessment hedged where appropriate and firm where justified; analytical register.

**Model paragraph** (reviewing the Dickens opening studied in Lesson 6):

> Dickens's famous opening is often quoted as a celebration of paradox; read closely, it is something sharper. The relentless antitheses, each pair canceling the last, do not describe 1775 so much as mock the confidence of anyone who would describe it at all. The narrator's stance is ironic rather than nostalgic: the epoch of belief and the epoch of incredulity are presented as the same epoch, and the reader is invited to recognize the pattern in "the present period". What the passage asserts, beneath its rhetoric, is a skepticism about historical judgment that the novel then spends four hundred pages complicating.

## Part 2: Assessing yourself honestly

Self-assessment fails through leniency (counting intention as achievement) or perfectionism (treating every slip as B2). Calibrate against the models in this course and published CPE sample scripts. Ask one near-native reader to mark only three things: the sentence they would cut, the word they would change and the idea they found most persuasive. Repeat the capstone every twelve months.

## Part 3: Maintaining C2 for life

Proficiency is a state, not a possession: without input and output, lexis narrows within two years. The plan below costs about five hours a week.

**Reading (2 hours).** One serious weekly periodical; one book a month alternating fiction and non-fiction, with a public-domain classic yearly; professional standards and journals in English (IFRS updates, *Journal of Accountancy*). Keep a notebook of near-synonyms and collocations, not translations.

**Listening (1.5 hours).** Podcasts with educated unscripted speech (*Freakonomics Radio*, *The Economist Podcasts*, BBC's *More or Less* and *In Our Time*). Watch one British and one American series without subtitles. Shadow five minutes a day.

**Speaking (1 hour).** Join an English-speaking professional community (an accountancy body's chapter, a Toastmasters club, an online debate society) and volunteer to present. Record a weekly two-minute monologue on an abstract noun.

**Writing (30 minutes).** Write something someone will read: a monthly LinkedIn article, a letter to the editor, comments on a draft standard. Edit with the Lesson 7 checklist; cut 20%.

**Communities.** Online: accounting and personal-finance forums for register and slang; ACCA and ICAEW forums for professional English; reading groups for literary discussion. Offline: conferences in English, where the Q&A is free speaking practice.

**Testing yourself.** Sit a real CPE or IELTS Academic every three to five years; the preparation matters more than the certificate.

| Armadilha | Antídoto |
|---|---|

| Falar só com brasileiros em inglês | Comunidade internacional |
| Fossilização de pequenos erros | Pedir correção específica |
| Escrever só e-mails curtos | Um texto de 800+ palavras por mês |

## Na prática

The capstone mirrors the deliverables of a senior accountant: the analytical report is a tax or audit review written for a board, the recorded talk is the client presentation of results, and the critical review is your reading of a new regulation or standard. Treat them as real work. Write the report about a genuine client situation, anonymized, and ask a colleague for the single cut and the single word change: {{en:Which sentence would you remove, and which word would you replace?}} Present the talk to your team before recording it. The maintenance plan becomes your professional routine: one serious text, one unscripted call and one written piece a week.

## Resumo

- The capstone has three parts: an 800-word analytical report with executive summary, headings and specified C2 structures; a self-recorded ten-minute talk with signposting, anticipated objections and Q&A; and a 400-word critical review of a complex text with attributed summary and evaluated stance.
- Score each task against the rubric (1–5 per criterion); C2 means 25/30 or more with no criterion below 4, and no register slips or comma splices.
- Self-assess by comparing with models and sample scripts, and by asking one reader for a single cut, a single word change and a single strongest idea.
- Maintain proficiency with roughly five hours a week: serious reading, unscripted listening with shadowing, a speaking community, and monthly writing that someone will read.
- Re-sit the capstone yearly and a formal exam every few years; proficiency is sustained by attention, volume and the willingness to be corrected.
