# Reading complex texts: literature, editorials and academic abstracts

A C2 reader understands abstract, structurally complex and highly colloquial texts and recognizes implicit meaning, attitude and stance. This lesson works through a literary excerpt (Dickens), a newspaper editorial and an academic abstract, teaching the inferencing each requires, and then turns to summarizing and paraphrasing at proficiency level: the core skills of CPE Reading Parts 5–7 and IELTS Reading at band 8.5+.

> **Glossário de apoio:** *inference* = inferência; *implied meaning* = sentido implícito; *stance* = posicionamento; *connotation* = conotação; *to paraphrase* = parafrasear; *to summarize* = resumir; *gist* = ideia geral; *to skim / to scan* = ler rapidamente para a ideia geral / para localizar informação; *to distort* = distorcer.

## 1. Three levels of reading

1. **Literal**: what the text says. (*Profits fell by 12%.*)
2. **Inferential**: what it implies. (*Calling the CEO's explanation "creative" implies it is not credible.*)
3. **Evaluative**: the writer's purpose and stance, and whether the argument holds.

C2 exam questions are overwhelmingly at levels 2 and 3: the answer is **built** from word choice, structure and tone, never copied.

## 2. Literary excerpt: Dickens

Charles Dickens, *A Tale of Two Cities* (1859, public domain), opening:

> It was the best of times, it was the worst of times, it was the age of wisdom, it was the age of foolishness, it was the epoch of belief, it was the epoch of incredulity, it was the season of Light, it was the season of Darkness, it was the spring of hope, it was the winter of despair, we had everything before us, we had nothing before us, we were all going direct to Heaven, we were all going direct the other way […]

**How to read it:**

- **Structure**: anaphora and antithesis in relentless pairs; the extract above is over eighty words, and in the original the sentence runs on for nearly 120.
- **Lexical register**: Latinate *epoch, incredulity* beside plain *best, worst*; the mixture signals irony.
- **Inference**: the pairs cancel each other, implying an age of contradiction and a narrator who distrusts simple judgments. *Direct the other way* is a comic euphemism for Hell.
- **Stance**: ironic, panoramic, mocking those who see their own age as unique (the period was, Dickens adds, "like the present period").

{{en:The pairs cancel each other out, suggesting an age of contradiction rather than of progress.}}

Literary reading at C2 requires tolerance for long sentences and the ability to hold a metaphor across a paragraph; read one public-domain novel a year alongside contemporary non-fiction.

## 3. The editorial

Separate **claim**, **evidence**, **assumption** and **rhetoric**.

Extract (fictitious):

> The Treasury's decision to freeze the audit threshold is being sold as a favor to small business. It is nothing of the kind. By leaving the threshold untouched while inflation pushes thousands of firms above it, the Treasury has engineered a stealth expansion of mandatory audit: more fees for the big firms, more paperwork for everyone else, and not a single additional dollar of protection for the public. Officials will say that audit builds confidence. So it does, when it is proportionate. A mandatory audit of a 40-person engineering firm builds nothing but the auditor's revenue. If the government genuinely believed in reducing burdens, it would index the threshold to inflation, as it does with almost every other limit in the tax code. That it has chosen not to tells us all we need to know about whose interests are being served.

**Reading it critically:**

- **Claim**: the freeze is a disguised expansion of mandatory audit benefiting large audit firms.
- **Evidence**: inflation pushes firms above a fixed threshold (true in principle); other limits are indexed (verifiable).
- **Assumption**: audits of small firms provide no public benefit (contestable; not argued, asserted).
- **Rhetoric**: *being sold as* (implies deception), *stealth* (negative connotation), tricolon *more fees… more paperwork… not a single…*, concession-then-attack (*So it does, when it is proportionate*), the final insinuation (*whose interests are being served*) without naming anyone.
- **Stance**: hostile, confident, populist. Signaled by *nothing of the kind*, *engineered*, *tells us all we need to know*.

Inference question: *What does the writer imply about the Treasury and large audit firms?* The correct inference is "that the policy serves the firms' interests", not "that there is proven corruption" (over-inference) nor "that the Treasury made a mistake" (under-inference).

## 4. The academic abstract

Abstracts are dense, hedged and conventional. Learn the moves: **background, gap, method, findings, implications**.

> Prior research has established a robust association between audit committee independence and financial reporting quality, yet the mechanisms underlying this association remain poorly understood. Drawing on a hand-collected dataset of 1,840 firm-years across three jurisdictions, we exploit a 2018 regulatory change that exogenously increased independence requirements. We find that the effect on reporting quality is concentrated in firms with pre-existing weak internal controls and is statistically indistinguishable from zero elsewhere. These results suggest that independence operates as a substitute for, rather than a complement to, internal monitoring. We caution, however, that our measure of reporting quality relies on discretionary accruals, whose validity has been questioned. Implications for regulators seeking to calibrate governance requirements are discussed.

**Decoding:** *robust association* = correlation, not causation; *remain poorly understood* = the gap; *exogenously* = from outside the firms' choices, supporting a causal reading; *indistinguishable from zero elsewhere* = the effect exists only for weak-control firms; *substitute for, rather than a complement to* = the key conceptual finding; *We caution… whose validity has been questioned* = a hedge that weakens the claim; *are discussed* = read the paper.

{{en:The authors claim a causal effect but immediately concede that their outcome measure is contested.}}

## 5. Inference strategies

Read **word choice** (*claimed* vs *showed*, *so-called*, scare quotes), **modality** (hedges signal uncertainty; their absence signals confidence), **what is not said** (a chairman who praises "resilience" and never mentions profit), **concessions** (*Admittedly…* reveals what the writer fears), **structure** (the point placed last is the one meant to land) and **tone words** (*merely, naturally, of course, inevitably* often carry irony).

## 6. Summarizing at C2

A summary is shorter, faithful and in your own words: identify the thesis and its two or three supporting moves, discard examples and rhetoric, and write in neutral register, attributing stance to the author (*The writer argues / contends / concedes*).

Summary of the editorial in 40 words: {{en:The writer argues that freezing the audit threshold while inflation rises amounts to a hidden expansion of mandatory audit that benefits large firms without protecting the public, and implies that the government's refusal to index the threshold reveals its true priorities.}}

The summary reports claim, mechanism, evaluation and insinuation without adopting the writer's tone.

## 7. Paraphrasing without plagiarism

A genuine paraphrase changes **structure**, **vocabulary** and **grammatical class** while preserving meaning.

Original: *We find that the effect on reporting quality is concentrated in firms with pre-existing weak internal controls.*

- Too close: *We find the effect on reporting quality concentrated in firms with weak internal controls.*
- Good: *Only companies whose internal controls were already weak showed any improvement in the quality of their reporting.*

Techniques: active ↔ passive; verb ↔ noun; clause ↔ phrase; reordering. Always cite the source when the idea is someone else's.

## Reading: critical text

Apply the full method to this fictitious magazine column.

> There is a particular kind of silence that falls over a boardroom when the finance director says the word "restatement". It is not the silence of shock; everyone present has suspected for months. It is the silence of people calculating. The chairman is calculating his legacy. The non-executives are calculating their exposure. The chief executive, who has spent three years describing the company as "disciplined", is calculating how many more times he can use the word before someone laughs. And the finance director, who has at last said what the numbers have been saying all along, is calculating nothing. He is simply, for the first time in a year, able to breathe. Restatements are treated as failures of accounting. More often they are failures of courage, delayed until the cost of silence exceeds the cost of speech. The accounting was fine. It was the listening that was broken.

Questions: What does the repetition of *calculating* imply about the board's priorities? Why is the finance director "calculating nothing"? What is the writer's thesis, stated in the final three sentences? Summarize the column in 30 words, attributing the stance. Which single word carries the heaviest irony?

## Na prática

Inferential reading is what an accountant does with a tax ruling, a regulator's editorial or a client's board minutes: the literal content is clear; the stance and the omission are the point. Read a ruling for what it concedes and what it leaves unsaid; read an abstract on tax policy for the hedge that weakens the headline: {{en:The reform is associated with higher compliance, though the effect is indistinguishable from zero in small firms.}} When you summarize for a client, attribute and neutralize: {{en:The author contends that the exemption primarily benefits larger companies.}} Paraphrase by restructuring, never by swapping synonyms into the original sentence.

## Resumo

- Read at three levels: literal, inferential, evaluative. C2 questions test what is implied and why, never what is copied.
- Literary prose (Dickens) demands tolerance for long parallel sentences, attention to register mixing and recognition of irony and euphemism.
- Editorials must be split into claim, evidence, assumption and rhetoric; stance is revealed by connotation (*stealth, engineered*), concession and insinuation.
- Academic abstracts follow background, gap, method, findings, implications; decode *robust association, exogenous, indistinguishable from zero* and weigh every hedge.
- Summarize by extracting thesis and moves in neutral register with attribution (*the writer contends*); paraphrase by changing structure, vocabulary and word class, not just synonyms.
