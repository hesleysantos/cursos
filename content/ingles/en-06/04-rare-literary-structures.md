# Rare and literary structures: subjunctive, archaic forms, complex noun phrases, stacked clauses and punctuation for effect

This lesson covers the structures that mark the top of the proficiency scale: the mandative and formulaic subjunctive, archaic and highly formal forms you will meet in legal and literary texts, dense noun phrases, the stacking of non-finite clauses, and punctuation used deliberately for rhythm and emphasis. You will not need to produce all of these daily, but a C2 reader must decode them instantly and a C2 writer must be able to deploy them when the genre calls for it.

> **Glossário de apoio:** *subjunctive* = subjuntivo; *mandative* = de ordem/exigência (após *insist, demand*); *formulaic* = em expressões fixas; *archaic* = arcaico; *non-finite clause* = oração sem verbo conjugado (infinitivo, particípio); *stacking* = empilhamento; *premodifier / postmodifier* = modificador antes / depois do núcleo.

## 1. The mandative subjunctive

After verbs, adjectives and nouns expressing demand, recommendation or necessity, formal English (especially American) uses the **base form** of the verb for all persons, with no *-s*, no tense and *not* placed before the verb.

Triggers: *insist, demand, require, recommend, suggest, propose, request, urge, ask, stipulate*; *it is essential / vital / imperative / crucial / important that*; *the recommendation / requirement / proposal that*.

- {{en:The auditors insisted that the provision be increased.}} (not *is increased*)
- {{en:It is essential that every transaction be documented.}}
- *The regulator requires that each director disclose any conflict of interest.*
- Negative: {{en:We recommend that the payment not be released until the goods arrive.}}
- Passive: *It was proposed that the meeting be adjourned.*

British English often prefers *should + infinitive* (*insisted that the provision should be increased*) or, informally, the indicative (*insisted that the provision was increased*, which can be ambiguous: did they insist it be increased, or insist that it had been?). In formal international writing, the subjunctive is the safest and most elegant choice.

Note the trap with *suggest*: *I suggest that he take the course* (recommendation, subjunctive) vs *The evidence suggests that he took the course* (implication, indicative).

## 2. The formulaic subjunctive and *were*

Fixed expressions preserve old subjunctive forms:

- *God save the King. Long live the Republic. Heaven forbid. Be that as it may. So be it. Far be it from me to criticize. Suffice it to say… Come what may. If need be. Perish the thought. Would that it were so.*
- {{en:Be that as it may, the deadline remains unchanged.}} — Seja como for…
- {{en:Suffice it to say that the negotiations did not go well.}} — Basta dizer que…
- *Were*-subjunctive: *If I were you…; as it were* (= so to speak); *were it not for…* (Module 5).

## 3. Archaic and highly formal forms

You will meet these in contracts, statutes, older literature, ceremonial language and deliberately elevated prose.

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| hereby, herein, hereof, hereto, hereinafter | by this document / in this / of this / to this / later in this | *The parties hereby agree…* |
| thereby, therein, thereof, thereafter, thereto | by that / in that / of that / after that / to that | *…and the costs thereof* |
| whereby, wherein, whereof, whereupon | by which / in which / of which / after which | *a scheme whereby employees acquire shares* |
| notwithstanding | despite | *Notwithstanding clause 4, …* |
| forthwith | immediately | *The license shall be returned forthwith.* |
| aforesaid / aforementioned | mentioned above | *the aforesaid premises* |
| save (for) | except | *All claims save those listed below…* |
| albeit | although | *a small, albeit significant, increase* |
| lest | for fear that | *He kept copies lest the originals be lost.* (subjunctive follows) |
| ere, whilst, amongst, amidst | before, while, among, amid | literary / formal UK (US: *while, among, amid*) |
| thou, thee, thy, -eth, -est | you (singular), your, verb endings | Shakespeare, King James Bible |
| methinks, prithee, anon | I think, please, soon | archaic literary |

{{en:The tenant shall, notwithstanding any prior agreement, vacate the premises forthwith upon receipt of notice.}}

Also literary: *Nor was this the only problem.*, *Many a time* (+ singular), *Woe betide anyone who…*, *Little wonder that…*, *Such being the case, …*, *Hence the delay.*

## 4. Complex noun phrases

Academic and legal English packs information into noun phrases rather than clauses. A noun phrase can carry **premodifiers** (determiners, adjectives, nouns) and **postmodifiers** (prepositional phrases, relative clauses, participle clauses, infinitives, appositives).

Example built step by step:

1. *the proposal*
2. *the controversial proposal*
3. *the board's controversial cost-reduction proposal*
4. *the board's controversial cost-reduction proposal, rejected by shareholders in May,*
5. {{en:The board's controversial cost-reduction proposal, rejected by shareholders in May and now revived in modified form, is to be debated again next week.}}

The whole 16-word phrase is the subject of *is*. Reading skill: find the head noun (*proposal*) and the main verb (*is to be debated*) first; everything else is modification. Writing skill: do not exceed roughly three premodifying nouns (*customer data protection policy review* is already hard) and keep the verb within sight of the head noun.

Noun-noun strings in finance: *year-end inventory valuation adjustment*, *deferred tax asset recognition criteria*, *related-party transaction disclosure requirements*. These are standard but should be unpacked for non-specialists: *the criteria for recognizing deferred tax assets*.

## 5. Stacking non-finite clauses

Literary and formal prose stacks participle and infinitive clauses to create rhythm and compression.

- {{en:Exhausted by the negotiations, unwilling to concede further, yet aware that walking away would cost more, the delegation asked for an adjournment.}} (three stacked clauses before the subject)
- *Having read the report, having consulted counsel and having slept on it, she signed.* (anaphoric stacking)
- *The firm, founded in 1920, run by the same family for three generations and now facing its first external audit, has little experience of scrutiny.* (stacked postmodifiers)
- Absolute clauses (noun + participle, no connector): {{en:The audit complete, the team returned to London.}} / *All things considered, the result was fair.* / *Weather permitting, the ceremony will be outdoors.*

Limit: stacked clauses must share the same implied subject (Module 5, dangling participles) and should not exceed three before the reader meets the main verb.

## 6. Punctuation for effect

At C2, punctuation is a stylistic instrument, not just a rule set.

**Semicolon (;)** links two independent clauses that are closely related, often in balance or contrast, and separates items in a complex list.

- {{en:Revenue rose; costs rose faster.}}
- *The delegates came from Lisbon, Portugal; Porto Alegre, Brazil; and Luanda, Angola.*

**Colon (:)** introduces an explanation, a list or a dramatic reveal; what follows explains what precedes.

- {{en:The committee reached one conclusion: the data could not be trusted.}}
- *Three factors drove the loss: currency, demand and fraud.*

**Dash (—)** adds an interruption, an afterthought or emphasis; paired dashes are a louder alternative to parentheses, a single dash a more dramatic alternative to a colon. US style uses an unspaced em dash (—); UK style often uses a spaced en dash ( – ).

- *The merger—the largest in the sector's history—collapsed in a week.*
- {{en:There was only one person who could have authorized the transfer—the CFO.}}

**Parentheses ( )** (UK: *brackets*) downgrade information: *The fee (payable quarterly) is non-refundable.*

**Short sentences and fragments** for impact: *The forecast was wrong. Badly wrong.* Fragments are acceptable in journalism and persuasive writing, never in reports.

**Oxford comma**: *currency, demand, and fraud* (US style) vs *currency, demand and fraud* (most UK style). Be consistent; add it when ambiguity threatens.

## Reading: critical text

The opening of Jane Austen's *Pride and Prejudice* (1813, public domain) is a masterclass in formal syntax and irony. Read it and analyze the structures.

> It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife. However little known the feelings or views of such a man may be on his first entering a neighbourhood, this truth is so well fixed in the minds of the surrounding families, that he is considered the rightful property of some one or other of their daughters.

Questions: Identify the complex noun phrase in the first sentence and its head noun. What structure is *However little known… may be*? (A concessive clause, "no matter how little known", with the complement *little known* fronted before the subject and verb.) Where does Austen's punctuation differ from modern usage, and why? What is ironic about *rightful property*? Rewrite the second sentence in plain modern English and note what is lost.

## Na prática

These structures live in the contracts, bylaws and legal opinions an accountant reads every month. The mandative subjunctive is standard in resolutions and audit recommendations: {{en:The board requires that the reserve be restated before the accounts are approved.}} Archaic connectives must be decoded instantly in a lease or a loan agreement: {{en:Notwithstanding the foregoing, the lessee shall remain liable for all taxes thereon.}} When you write, limit yourself to the forms the genre expects, locate the head noun in every dense noun phrase, and use a semicolon or a dash only when it genuinely clarifies the relationship between the figures.

## Resumo

- The mandative subjunctive (base form after *insist, demand, recommend, it is essential that*) is standard in formal and American English; negatives put *not* before the base verb (*that the payment not be released*).
- Formulaic subjunctives (*be that as it may, suffice it to say, come what may, if need be*) and *were* survive in fixed phrases.
- Archaic and legal forms (*hereby, thereof, whereby, notwithstanding, forthwith, save for, lest, albeit*) must be decoded instantly; use them only in the genres that expect them.
- Complex noun phrases and stacked non-finite clauses compress information; locate the head noun and main verb when reading, and limit premodifying nouns and stacked clauses when writing.
- Semicolons balance clauses, colons introduce and reveal, dashes interrupt and emphasize; choose one style for the Oxford comma and keep it.
