# Lexis I: idioms, three-part phrasal verbs, binomials, metaphor and register

This lesson expands your lexical range in the directions that matter most at C1: idiomatic expressions used by educated speakers, phrasal verbs with three parts, binomial pairs, the metaphors that structure business English, and the ability to choose between formal, neutral and informal synonyms according to register. The CAE Use of English paper tests exactly these areas, and IELTS examiners reward "less common lexical items used with awareness of style and collocation".

> **Glossário de apoio:** *idiom* = expressão idiomática; *three-part phrasal verb* = verbo frasal com partícula + preposição (*put up with*); *binomial* = par fixo de palavras ligado por *and/or* (*terms and conditions*); *connotation* = conotação (carga emocional/avaliativa); *register* = registro (nível de formalidade).

## 1. Idioms that educated professionals actually use

Avoid textbook idioms that native speakers rarely say (*it's raining cats and dogs*). Focus on idioms that occur in business journalism and meetings.

| Idiom | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| the bottom line | the final result; the essential point | {{en:The bottom line is that we cannot afford another delay.}} |
| cut corners | do something cheaply or carelessly | *The contractor cut corners on safety.* |
| in the pipeline | being planned or developed | {{en:Two new products are in the pipeline for next year.}} |
| a ballpark figure | a rough estimate | *Can you give me a ballpark figure for the renovation?* |
| get the ball rolling | start something | *Let's get the ball rolling with introductions.* |
| on the back burner | postponed, low priority | *The expansion plan has been put on the back burner.* |
| touch base | make brief contact | *I'll touch base with the client next week.* |
| a level playing field | fair conditions for all | {{en:Small firms are calling for a level playing field on tax.}} |
| in the red / in the black | making a loss / a profit | *The division has been in the red for three quarters.* |
| the elephant in the room | an obvious problem nobody mentions | *The pension deficit is the elephant in the room.* |
| move the goalposts | change the rules unfairly | *The regulator keeps moving the goalposts.* |
| take something with a grain of salt (UK: a pinch of salt) | not believe entirely | *Take their forecasts with a grain of salt.* |
| at the end of the day | ultimately (spoken, overused) | *At the end of the day, the client decides.* |
| call it a day | stop working | *Let's call it a day and resume tomorrow.* |

## 2. Three-part phrasal verbs

These combine a verb, an adverb particle and a preposition. They are inseparable: the object always follows the whole unit.

- **put up with** = tolerate: {{en:We cannot put up with late payments indefinitely.}}
- **get away with** = escape punishment: *They got away with under-reporting for years.*
- **look forward to** + -ing: *We look forward to working with you.*
- **come up with** = produce (an idea): {{en:The team came up with a workable solution within hours.}}
- **run out of** = exhaust the supply: *We have run out of time.*
- **get around to** (UK also: *get round to*) = finally do: *I never got around to filing those receipts.*
- **live up to** = meet (expectations): *The product failed to live up to the hype.*
- **do away with** = abolish: *The reform did away with paper invoices.*
- **keep up with** = stay at the same pace: *Small firms struggle to keep up with regulatory change.*
- **face up to** = confront: *The board must face up to the losses.*
- **boil down to** = be essentially: {{en:The dispute boils down to a disagreement about depreciation methods.}}
- **cut back on** = reduce: *We have cut back on travel.*
- **get on with** = continue; (UK) have a good relationship (US: *get along with*): *Let's get on with the agenda.*
- **stand up for / stand up to** = defend / resist: *She stood up to the auditors.*
- **fall back on** = use as a reserve: *We can fall back on the credit line.*

## 3. Binomials

Binomials are fixed pairs in a fixed order. Reversing them (~~conditions and terms~~) sounds wrong. Many belong to legal and financial English.

- *terms and conditions*, *profit and loss*, *assets and liabilities*, *supply and demand*, *trial and error*, *wear and tear*, *pros and cons*, *checks and balances*, *null and void*, *law and order*, *by and large* (= generally), *first and foremost*, *sooner or later*, *more or less*, *give and take*, *ups and downs*, *safe and sound*, *loud and clear*, *part and parcel* (= an integral part), *odds and ends*, *peace and quiet*, *back and forth*.

{{en:Regular wear and tear is not covered by the warranty.}} / {{en:Compliance is part and parcel of running a listed company.}}

## 4. Metaphor in business English

Business English is built on a handful of conceptual metaphors. Recognizing them helps you understand and produce idiomatic language in coherent clusters.

| Metaphor | Vocabulary cluster |
|---|---|
| Business is **war** | *target, strategy, campaign, capture market share, price war, hostile takeover, launch an attack on costs* |
| Business is **sport** | *level playing field, move the goalposts, ballpark, team player, front-runner, a game changer* |
| The economy is **a body / health** | *healthy profits, a sick company, recovery, ailing sector, inject capital, financial hemorrhage* |
| Money is **liquid** | *cash flow, liquidity, pour money into, drain resources, frozen assets, a trickle of orders, flooded the market* |
| Growth is **upward movement** | *soar, plummet, peak, bottom out, rally, slump, skyrocket, nosedive* |
| A company is **a ship / journey** | *steer the company, weather the storm, on course, change tack, run aground, a roadmap* |

{{en:After a turbulent year, the company is finally back on course and weathering the storm better than its rivals.}}

## 5. Connotation

Words with similar denotation may carry different evaluations. Choosing the wrong one changes your message.

| Positive | Neutral | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| thrifty, economical | careful with money | stingy, tight-fisted, mean (UK) |
| assertive, confident | direct | aggressive, pushy, bossy |
| determined, resolute | persistent | stubborn, obstinate, pig-headed |
| slim, slender | thin | skinny, scrawny |
| inquisitive, curious | interested | nosy |
| youthful | young | immature, childish |
| cautious, prudent | careful | timid, indecisive |
| statesman | politician | careerist |

{{en:Describing the finance director as prudent rather than timid completely changes the tone of the appraisal.}}

## 6. Register: formal, neutral, informal synonyms

| Informal | Neutral | Formal |
|---|---|---|
| kick off | start | commence |
| put off | postpone | defer |
| find out | discover | ascertain |
| get in touch | contact | liaise with |
| go up | increase | rise / appreciate (prices, assets) |
| a lot of | many | numerous / a considerable number of |
| get rid of | remove | eliminate / dispose of |
| think about | consider | deliberate on / give consideration to |
| ask for | request | solicit |
| help | assist | facilitate |
| need | require | necessitate |
| buy | purchase | acquire / procure |

{{en:We regret to inform you that the meeting has been deferred until further notice.}} (formal) vs *Sorry, the meeting's been put off.* (informal)

Register is also marked by contractions (informal), phrasal verbs (informal/neutral), Latinate vocabulary (formal), passive voice (formal) and sentence length.

## 7. Typical errors made by Portuguese speakers

| Erro | Correção | Comentário |
|---|---|---|
| ~~I look forward to hear from you.~~ | I look forward to hearing from you. | *To* é preposição; pede -ing. |
| ~~We came up a solution.~~ | We came up with a solution. | Verbo de três partes é inseparável. |
| ~~conditions and terms~~ | terms and conditions | Binomial tem ordem fixa. |
| ~~He is very economic.~~ | He is very economical / thrifty. | *Economic* = relativo à economia. |
| ~~She is an aggressive saleswoman~~ (como elogio) | She is an assertive saleswoman. | Aplicado a pessoas, *aggressive* costuma ser negativo (hostil, agressivo). |
| ~~Pretend~~ para "pretender" | intend | *Pretend* = fingir. |
| ~~I'll touch the base with you.~~ | I'll touch base with you. | Expressão fixa, sem artigo. |
| Misturar registros: ~~We hereby kick off the audit~~ | We hereby commence the audit. | Formal + informal na mesma frase soa estranho. |

## Reading: critical text

Read the extract from a business magazine and identify the metaphors, idioms and binomials. Then consider whether the writer's figurative language clarifies or obscures the facts.

> By and large, the retail sector weathered the first wave of inflation surprisingly well. Margins were squeezed, but most chains kept their heads above water by cutting back on marketing and putting expansion plans on the back burner. The elephant in the room, however, was debt. Having borrowed heavily when money was cheap, several mid-sized retailers now find themselves in the red, hemorrhaging cash to service loans whose terms and conditions were negotiated in a different world. Analysts who insisted that the sector would bounce back are quietly moving the goalposts, talking of "stabilization" rather than recovery. The bottom line is that trial and error has replaced strategy. Firms that came up with genuine innovations are keeping up; those that cut corners are falling behind. Sooner or later the market will separate the two, and no amount of upbeat language will change that.

Questions: Which metaphor dominates the paragraph? Which idiom is used critically about analysts? Rewrite the final sentence in plain, literal English.

## Na prática

Idioms and register choices show up the moment you speak with an English-speaking client or partner firm. Used sparingly, professional idioms make you sound at ease: {{en:The bottom line is that the restructuring is still in the pipeline.}} The register switch matters more: *put off* with a colleague becomes *defer* in a letter to the tax authority, and *figure out* becomes *determine*. Binomials appear in every engagement letter (*terms and conditions, profit and loss*). Before sending anything, scan for mixed registers and for quaint textbook idioms; a single *raining cats and dogs* undoes a carefully built professional image.

## Resumo

- Learn idioms that professionals actually use (*the bottom line, in the pipeline, a level playing field, move the goalposts*) and avoid quaint textbook ones.
- Three-part phrasal verbs (*put up with, come up with, get around to, boil down to*) are inseparable and often take -ing (*look forward to hearing*).
- Binomials have fixed order: *terms and conditions, profit and loss, wear and tear, part and parcel*.
- Business English runs on metaphors of war, sport, health, liquid and movement; recognizing the cluster helps you understand new expressions.
- Choose synonyms by connotation (*prudent* vs *timid*) and by register (*put off / postpone / defer*); do not mix registers in one sentence.
