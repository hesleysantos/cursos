# Listening and speaking at C1

This lesson explains why fast native speech is hard to understand even when you know every word, and what to do about it. You will study connected speech (linking, elision, assimilation, weak forms), intonation as a carrier of attitude, strategies for coping with speed and accents, and then turn to production: structuring a presentation and handling questions under pressure. The listening sections of CAE and IELTS, and the Speaking Part 2 long turn, test exactly these competences.

> **Glossário de apoio:** *connected speech* = fala encadeada; *linking* = ligação entre palavras; *elision* = elisão (supressão de som); *assimilation* = assimilação (um som muda por influência do vizinho); *weak form* = forma fraca (vogal reduzida, schwa /ə/); *stress-timed* = ritmo acentual (sílabas tônicas em intervalos regulares); *intonation* = entonação.

## 1. Why native speech sounds like a single word

English is **stress-timed**: stressed syllables occur at roughly regular intervals and everything between them is compressed. Portuguese is closer to syllable-timed, so Brazilians instinctively expect every syllable to be audible. In {{en:I should have told them about it}}, a native speaker stresses only *told* and *about*; the rest is squeezed into weak, linked, partly deleted sounds: /aɪ ʃədəv ˈtoʊldəm əˈbaʊɾɪt/.

## 2. Connected speech processes

| Process | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Consonant-to-vowel linking** | final consonant joins the next vowel | *an_apple* → "a napple"; *pick_it_up* → "pi-ki-tup" |
| **Vowel-to-vowel linking** | /j/ or /w/ is inserted | *the_end* → "the-yend"; *go_on* → "go-won" |
| **Linking /r/** (UK) | silent final r is pronounced before a vowel | *far_away*, *the auditor_is here* |
| **Elision** | sounds disappear, especially /t/ and /d/ between consonants | *next week* → "nex week"; *must be* → "mus be"; *I don't know* → "I dunno" |
| **Assimilation** | a sound changes to resemble its neighbor | *ten people* → "tem people"; *good boy* → "gub boy"; *this year* → "thish year"; *would you* → "wouldja" |
| **Weak forms** | function words lose their full vowel | *to* /tə/, *for* /fər/, *and* /ən/, *can* /kən/, *have* /əv/, *that* /ðət/, *of* /əv/, *was* /wəz/ |
| **Contraction and reduction** | auxiliaries collapse | *should have* → "should've" → "shoulda"; *going to* → "gonna"; *want to* → "wanna" |

{{en:We could have done it a lot faster if they had told us earlier.}} In fast speech: "We coulda dunnit a lot faster if they'd toldus earlier."

Practical consequence: **listen for stressed content words and reconstruct the grammar from them.** The function words are predictable; your knowledge of grammar fills them in.

## 3. Weak forms in detail

Around forty common words have strong and weak forms; the strong form appears only when the word is stressed, final or contrasted.

- *can*: weak /kən/ in *I can do it*; strong /kæn/ in *Yes, I can.* (*Can't* is always strong.)
- *have*: weak /əv/ in *They have left*; strong as a main verb: *I have two.*
- *that*: weak /ðət/ as a conjunction; strong /ðæt/ as a demonstrative.
- *there*: weak /ðər/ in *there is*; strong /ðer/ for place.

## 4. Intonation and attitude

The same words can be sincere, sarcastic, doubtful or dismissive depending on pitch movement.

- **Fall** (↘): finality, certainty, statements, wh-questions. {{en:The meeting is at nine.}}
- **Rise** (↗): yes/no questions, incompleteness, politeness, surprise.
- **Fall-rise** (↘↗): reservation, "but…", polite disagreement, implication. *It's a good plan* ↘↗ = "…but I have doubts." This tone is extremely common in British speech and is often missed by learners, who hear agreement where there is hesitation.
- **Rise-fall**: strong emotion, irony, being impressed. *Really?!*
- **High level / flat**: boredom, sarcasm: *Great. Another meeting.*

Nuclear stress (the main stressed word in a tone unit) marks contrast and new information: *I didn't say HE took the money* vs *I didn't say he TOOK the money*. Each shift changes the implied meaning.

## 5. Coping with speed and accents

- **Accents you will meet:** General American (the reference accent of this course), RP and Estuary English (UK), Scottish, Irish, Australian, Indian English and international non-native English. Expect vowel shifts (Australian *day* sounds close to "die"), /r/ dropping in England and Australia (but not in Scotland or Ireland), /t/ flapping in the US (*water* → "wadder") and glottal stops in London speech (*bottle* → "bo'le").
- **Strategies:** predict content before listening; listen for stressed words and discourse markers; do not translate; tolerate ambiguity rather than freezing on one unknown word; in exams, read the questions first and listen for paraphrase.
- **Training routine:** 15 minutes a day of authentic audio, first without transcript, then with, then shadowing (repeating a second behind the speaker). Shadowing trains your brain to expect compressed forms.

## 6. Giving a structured presentation

A C1 speaker can talk for ten minutes without notes and without losing the audience. The structure below is standard in business and in exam long turns.

1. **Hook and purpose** (30 s): a question, a figure, a short anecdote; then {{en:What I'd like to do today is outline three options and recommend one.}}
2. **Roadmap**: *I'll start with… then move on to… and finish with…*
3. **Body**: one idea per section, each with a signpost (Lesson 7), an example and a mini-summary. Use **contrast** and **triads** for memorability: *cheaper, faster, and far less risky.*
4. **Conclusion**: restate the recommendation, state the next step, stop. Do not introduce new material.
5. **Delivery**: pause after key points; vary pace; stress content words; keep sentences short; use *you* and *we*.

## 7. Handling Q&A

- **Buying time:** {{en:That's a very good question, and it's one we considered carefully.}} / *Let me make sure I've understood: you're asking whether…*
- **Clarifying:** *Could you be more specific about which period you mean?*
- **Admitting limits honestly:** *I don't have that figure on hand, but I'll get it to you by this afternoon.* (UK: *to hand*)
- **Deflecting hostility:** *I understand the concern. The way I'd look at it is…* / *That's a fair challenge. Two points in response…*
- **Bridging back:** *Which actually brings us back to the central issue of…*
- **Closing a question:** *Does that answer your question?* / *Happy to discuss further afterward.*
- **Interrupting politely:** *Sorry to cut in, but…* / *Could I just finish this point?*

## 8. Fluency devices for speaking exams

Native-sounding fillers (*well, I mean, the thing is, to be honest*), paraphrase when a word escapes you (*it's a kind of…*), speculation for pictures (*It looks as though… / Judging by… / I'd imagine…*) and evaluation frames (*Whereas…, on balance I'd go for…*).

## 9. Typical errors made by Portuguese speakers

| Erro | Correção | Comentário |
|---|---|---|
| Pronunciar todas as sílabas com a mesma força | Reduzir as palavras gramaticais | Inglês é stress-timed; sem redução, soa robótico e você não entende os nativos. |
| *can* e *can't* confundidos | *can* fraco /kən/; *can't* forte /kɑːnt/ (UK) ou /kænt/ (US) | A diferença é mais de vogal que de /t/, que muitas vezes nem se ouve. |
| Vogal de apoio no final: *big-e*, *book-e* | Terminar na consoante | Interfere no linking. |
| Entonação ascendente em afirmações | Fall para afirmações | Soa inseguro ou como pergunta. |
| Ouvir fall-rise como concordância | Reconhecer reserva | "It's a good idea" ↘↗ = "mas…". |
| ~~I will present about…~~ | I'll be talking about… / I'd like to present… | *Present* é transitivo. |
| ~~Thanks for your question.~~ repetido a cada pergunta | Variar: *Good question / Let me take that one / That's a fair point* | Fórmula repetida soa mecânica. |

## Reading: critical text

Read the transcript of the opening of a presentation and evaluate it. What does the speaker do well, and where would a C1 examiner deduct marks?

> Good morning, everyone, and thank you for making the time. I want to begin with a number: forty-one percent. That's the proportion of our invoices that were paid late last year. So what I'd like to do in the next ten minutes is look at why that happened, show you what it cost us, and then propose three practical changes. I'll take questions at the end, if that's all right. Let's start with the why. Broadly speaking, there are two causes, one structural and one behavioral, and I'll deal with each in turn. On the structural side, as the chart on the left shows, our credit terms vary enormously from client to client, which, frankly, makes it almost impossible to chase debts consistently. Now, some of you may be thinking that's simply the nature of the business, and to an extent I'd agree. But, and this is the key point, the variation isn't deliberate; it's historical. Nobody decided it. It just happened. And that brings me to the behavioral cause…

Questions: Identify the hook, the roadmap and two signposts. Where does the speaker anticipate an objection? Which sentences would carry a fall-rise intonation?

## Na prática

Connected speech is the reason a conference call with an American client feels faster than the slides. Expect *going to* as *gonna* and *did you* as *didja*; listen for the stressed content words and let the figures carry the sentence. When you present the quarterly results, build the talk with a roadmap and signposts: {{en:I'll start with revenue, then move on to costs, and finish with the cash position.}} In Q&A, buy time and bridge back: {{en:That's a fair question; let me come back to the provision figures.}} Shadow one earnings call a week to train both ears and mouth.

## Resumo

- English is stress-timed; function words are reduced to weak forms and linked, elided or assimilated, which is why fluent speech sounds like one long word. Listen for stressed content words and let grammar fill the gaps.
- Key processes: linking (*an_apple*), elision (*nex week*), assimilation (*wouldja*), weak forms (*to* /tə/, *can* /kən/, *have* /əv/).
- Intonation carries attitude: fall = certainty; rise = question or politeness; fall-rise = reservation ("but…"); nuclear stress marks contrast.
- Cope with speed and accents by predicting, tolerating ambiguity, listening for paraphrase and training with shadowing.
- A C1 presentation has a hook, a roadmap, signposted sections with examples, and a conclusion with a next step; in Q&A, buy time, clarify, admit limits honestly and bridge back to your message.
