# Advanced conditionals and hypothetical language

You already know the four "textbook" conditionals. This lesson takes you into the territory that separates B2 from C1: mixed conditionals, *were to*, *if it weren't for*, *otherwise*, *supposing*, *as if / as though*, *would rather / would sooner* and *it's (high) time*. All of these express unreality, distance or politeness, and they are heavily tested in CAE Key Word Transformations and rewarded in IELTS Writing for grammatical range.

> **Glossário de apoio:** *hypothetical* = hipotético; *unreal past* = passado irreal (forma de passado com sentido de irrealidade, não de tempo); *backshift* = recuo do tempo verbal.

## 1. The core principle: distance, not time

In hypothetical language, the past tense does not mean "past". It means **distance from reality**. *If I had more time* refers to now; *If I had had more time* refers to then. Once you internalize this, every structure in this lesson becomes predictable:

- Present/future unreality → past simple (*were, had, knew*).
- Past unreality → past perfect (*had been, had known*).

## 2. Mixed conditionals

Mixed conditionals combine a condition in one time frame with a result in another. There are two common patterns.

**Past condition → present result** (the most frequent):

- {{en:If the company had invested in training, it would have a stronger team today.}} — Se tivesse investido…, teria hoje…
- {{en:Had we not diversified in 2019, we would be in serious trouble now.}}

**Present condition → past result** (a permanent state explains a past event):

- {{en:If she were more cautious, she would not have signed that contract.}} — Se ela fosse mais cautelosa, não teria assinado…
- {{en:If I didn't trust you, I wouldn't have given you access to the accounts.}}

A third, less common pattern mixes a past condition with a future result: *If I had finished the report yesterday, I would be flying to Lisbon tomorrow.*

## 3. *Were to*, *should* and *happen to*

These make a condition sound more remote or more tentative, which is useful in negotiations.

| Structure | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|
| If + subject + were to + infinitive | very hypothetical, formal | {{en:If the central bank were to raise rates again, borrowing costs would soar.}} |
| Were + subject + to (inverted) | even more formal | *Were the client to withdraw, we would lose a third of our revenue.* |
| If + subject + should | "if by chance" | *If you should change your mind, the offer stands until Friday.* |
| If + subject + happen to | casual chance | *If you happen to see Marta, remind her about the invoice.* |

*Were to* is not used for impossible past conditions; it points to the future. Note also that *were to* with a first-type meaning does not exist: you cannot say ~~If it were to rain tomorrow, we will cancel~~ — the result clause needs *would*.

## 4. *If it weren't for / If it hadn't been for / But for*

These structures mean "without the influence of X".

- {{en:If it weren't for the tax incentive, the project would not be viable.}} — Não fosse o incentivo fiscal…
- {{en:If it hadn't been for your warning, we would have missed the deadline.}}
- Formal equivalent: *But for your warning, we would have missed the deadline.*
- Inverted: *Were it not for… / Had it not been for…*

## 5. *Otherwise*, *or else*, *in which case*, *in that case*

These connectors carry an implied condition and are extremely common in professional writing because they avoid long *if*-clauses.

- {{en:Submit the form by Friday; otherwise, the application will be rejected.}} (= if you don't, …)
- *Pay now, or else interest will accrue.*
- {{en:The supplier may miss the shipment date, in which case we will invoke the penalty clause.}} (= and if that happens, …)
- *The budget may be cut. In that case, we will postpone the launch.*

## 6. *Supposing*, *suppose*, *imagine*, *provided (that)*, *as long as*, *unless*, *even if*

- {{en:Supposing the merger fell through, what would our fallback position be?}} — Supondo que…
- *Imagine you were offered the CFO job tomorrow. Would you take it?*
- *Provided that the figures are verified, we can release the statement.* (= only if)
- *As long as the audit trail is intact, the regulator will be satisfied.*
- *Unless* = *if… not*: *Unless the contract is amended, we cannot proceed.* Do not add another negative: ~~Unless the contract isn't amended~~.
- *Even if* concedes: *Even if the data were perfect, the model would still be wrong.*

## 7. *As if / as though*

These compare reality with an unreal situation. The tense shows how unreal the comparison is.

- Real possibility: *It looks as if the market is recovering.* (maybe it is)
- Unreal: {{en:He talks as if he owned the company.}} (he doesn't)
- Past unreal: {{en:She behaved as though nothing had happened.}}
- Informal spoken English often uses *like*: *He talks like he owns the place.* Avoid this in formal writing.

## 8. *Would rather / would sooner / would prefer*

**Same subject** → bare infinitive: {{en:I would rather postpone the meeting than present incomplete figures.}}

**Different subject** → unreal past: {{en:I would rather you didn't mention the lawsuit to the client.}} — Preferiria que você não mencionasse… Past reference: *I'd rather you hadn't told them.*

*Would sooner* is a slightly more emphatic synonym: *I'd sooner resign than falsify the accounts.* *Would prefer* takes *to* + infinitive or *it if*: *I'd prefer it if you called first.*

## 9. *It's (high / about) time*

- {{en:It's high time the board addressed the pension deficit.}} — Já passou da hora de o conselho…
- *It's about time we updated the software.*
- Unreal past after *time*; alternative with *to*: *It's time to go.* (neutral, no criticism). *High time* implies criticism of delay.

## 10. *Wish* and *if only* at C1

- Regret about the present: *I wish the figures were clearer.* Past: *If only we had checked the exchange rate.*
- Annoyance about behavior / desire for change: *I wish you would stop interrupting.* (not for yourself: ~~I wish I would~~)
- Formal alternative: *We would have preferred the client to have been consulted.*

## 11. Typical errors made by Portuguese speakers

| Erro | Correção | Comentário |
|---|---|---|
| ~~If I would have known, I would have come.~~ | If I had known, I would have come. | *Would* não entra na oração condicional. |
| ~~I'd rather you don't call him.~~ | I'd rather you didn't call him. | Sujeito diferente pede passado irreal. |
| ~~It's time that we go.~~ | It's time we went. / It's time to go. | *It's time + subject* pede passado. |
| ~~Unless you don't pay, we will…~~ | Unless you pay, we will… | *Unless* já é negativo. |
| ~~As if he would be the boss.~~ | As if he were the boss. | Passado irreal, não *would*. |
| ~~If it wouldn't be for the loan…~~ | If it weren't for the loan… | Mesma regra. |
| ~~I wish I would have more time.~~ | I wish I had more time. | *Wish + would* só para outra pessoa/coisa. |

## Reading: critical text

Read this extract from an opinion piece and identify every hypothetical structure. Then ask yourself: does the author use hypotheticals to inform or to manipulate?

> Supposing the government had not bailed out the bank in 2008, what would the economy look like today? Economists are divided, but the dominant view is that, had the bank collapsed, the contagion would have spread far faster than it did. If it hadn't been for the rescue package, pension funds would have lost billions and ordinary savers would be poorer now. Critics respond as though the bailout were a gift to reckless executives; in fact, most of the money was repaid with interest. Were a similar crisis to occur tomorrow, the regulatory framework would respond differently, in which case the taxpayer would be far better protected. It is high time we stopped relitigating 2008 and started preparing for the next shock. Otherwise, we risk learning the wrong lesson from the right crisis. I would rather we debated the future than rehearsed the past.

Discussion: the author uses nine hypothetical structures in fewer than 150 words (*supposing… had not, had the bank collapsed, if it hadn't been for, as though… were, were… to occur, in which case, it is high time, otherwise, would rather we debated*). Which ones present speculation as fact? How does *as though* frame the critics?

## Na prática

Advanced conditionals are the language of advice and risk in an accounting office. A mixed conditional explains how a past decision shapes the present position: {{en:If the company had registered for the simplified regime, it would be paying far less tax now.}} *Were to* and *should* frame scenarios tactfully in client letters: {{en:Should the auditors request further evidence, we would need the original contracts.}} *Otherwise* and *in which case* keep a recommendation short and precise. Use these structures when the client needs to see a consequence clearly without feeling accused of having caused it.

## Resumo

- Hypothetical past tenses mark distance from reality, not time; mixed conditionals combine time frames (*If we had invested, we would have a stronger team now*).
- *Were to*, *should* and *happen to* make conditions more remote or tentative; *if it weren't / hadn't been for* and *but for* mean "without X".
- *Otherwise*, *or else*, *in which case* and *in that case* carry implied conditions and shorten professional prose.
- *As if / as though*, *would rather + different subject* and *it's (high) time* all take the unreal past, never *would*.
- *Unless* already contains the negative; never double it.
