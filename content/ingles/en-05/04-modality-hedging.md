# Advanced modality, hedging and diplomatic language

In this lesson you will refine your control of modal verbs and learn the language of hedging and diplomacy. At C1, modality is not about knowing that *must* is stronger than *should*; it is about distinguishing **epistemic** from **deontic** meaning, using expressions such as *may well*, *could hardly*, *is bound to* and *be to + infinitive*, and softening your message so that it persuades without offending. This is the grammar of negotiation, of auditors' letters and of any email in which you have to say "no" to a client.

> **Glossário de apoio:** *epistemic* = relativo à probabilidade/conhecimento (o que achamos que é verdade); *deontic* = relativo a obrigação, permissão e vontade; *hedging* = atenuar, "cercar" uma afirmação para não parecer categórico; *downtoner* = atenuador.

## 1. Epistemic vs deontic modality

Most modals have two faces. Compare:

| Modal | Deontic (obligation / permission) | Epistemic (probability) |
|---|---|---|
| must | *You must file by May 31.* (obrigação) | {{en:The invoice must have been sent to the wrong address.}} (dedução) |
| should | *You should keep receipts.* (conselho) | {{en:The payment should arrive by Friday.}} (expectativa) |
| may | *You may leave early.* (permissão) | *The rate may fall.* (possibilidade) |
| can't | *You can't park here.* (proibição) | {{en:That figure can't be right; it is ten times last year's.}} (dedução negativa) |
| will | *I will help.* (vontade) | *That will be the courier.* (certeza sobre o presente) |

In the past, epistemic modals take *have + past participle*: *must have been, can't have known, may have overlooked, should have arrived*. Deontic past is different: *had to, was allowed to, was supposed to*. (*Needn't have worried* also uses *have + past participle*, but it is about necessity, not probability; see section 4.)

Key distinction for Portuguese speakers: *must have* (dedução) ≠ *had to* (obrigação). "Ele deve ter esquecido" = *He must have forgotten*. "Ele teve de sair" = *He had to leave*.

## 2. Degrees of certainty: the C1 toolkit

From strongest to weakest:

1. {{en:The merger is bound to attract regulatory scrutiny.}} — certainly will (é certo que…)
2. *The results are sure to / certain to disappoint.*
3. {{en:Interest rates may well rise before the end of the year.}} — quite likely (*may well* = é bem possível que)
4. *The client could well reject the proposal.* / *might well*
5. *It is likely / probable / conceivable that…*
6. *The supplier could conceivably deliver early.*
7. *It is doubtful whether…* / *There is little chance that…*
8. {{en:We could hardly have predicted a 40% drop in demand.}} — almost impossible (*could hardly* = dificilmente poderíamos)
9. *There is no way the figures add up.* (informal)

Note the difference between *may well* (likelihood) and *might as well* (no better alternative: *We might as well cancel; nobody is coming*).

## 3. *Be to + infinitive* and related forms

- Formal arrangement or instruction: {{en:The CEO is to address the shareholders on Monday.}} / *All staff are to complete the training by June.*
- Destiny / later events in narrative: *She was to become the first woman to chair the committee.*
- In conditionals: *If we are to meet the deadline, we need two more analysts.* (= if we want to)
- *Was/were to have + past participle* for unrealized plans: *The audit was to have begun in March, but it was postponed.*
- *Be due to* (scheduled), *be about to* (imminent), *be bound to* (certain), *be liable to* (tends to, negative), *be supposed to* (expected, but perhaps not happening).

## 4. Other modal nuances

- *Need*: as a modal in negatives and questions (*You needn't attend*), as a full verb otherwise (*You don't need to attend*). *Needn't have done* = did it unnecessarily; *didn't need to do* = it was unnecessary (and probably not done).
- *Shall* in formal and legal registers = obligation: *The tenant shall pay rent monthly.* In questions, offers: *Shall we begin?*
- *Would* for habitual past and for distancing: *That would be the auditor's view, I imagine.*
- *Dare*: *I daren't ask for another extension.*
- *Ought to* ≈ *should*, slightly more formal, rare in negatives.

## 5. Hedging in writing

Academic and professional writing avoids absolute claims unless they are provable. Hedging devices:

- **Lexical verbs:** *appear, seem, tend to, suggest, indicate*: {{en:The data suggest that demand tends to peak in the fourth quarter.}}
- **Adverbs:** *arguably, apparently, presumably, generally, to some extent, broadly*.
- **Adjectives/nouns:** *a possible explanation, a likely cause, the assumption that…*
- **Impersonal frames:** *It could be argued that…*, *There is some evidence that…*, *As far as we can ascertain…*
- **Quantifiers:** *most, many, a number of, in the majority of cases*.

Contrast: *Automation eliminates jobs* (unhedged, easy to attack) vs {{en:Automation appears to reduce demand for certain categories of routine work, at least in the short term.}}

## 6. Diplomatic and softening language in negotiation

Native speakers of English, especially in British business culture, routinely understate. "I'm not sure that's quite right" often means "that's wrong". Learn both to produce and to decode these signals.

| Direct | Diplomatic |
|---|---|
| That's wrong. | {{en:I'm not sure that's entirely accurate.}} |
| We can't accept that price. | {{en:We would find it difficult to accept that price.}} / *That might be a little on the high side.* |
| You made a mistake. | *There seems to have been a slight misunderstanding.* |
| I disagree. | *I take your point, but I see it rather differently.* |
| Do it by Friday. | *Would it be possible to have this by Friday?* / *I wonder if you could…* |
| No. | *I'm afraid that wouldn't be feasible at this stage.* |
| You must sign. | *We would need your signature before we can proceed.* |

Techniques:

- **Past tense distancing:** *I was wondering whether…*, *We were hoping to…*, *I thought we might…*
- **Downtoners:** *a bit, slightly, somewhat, rather, a little, not quite*.
- **Negative question / tentative structures:** *Wouldn't it be better to…?*, *Might I suggest…?*
- **Agree before disagreeing:** *You're right that costs are a concern; however, …*
- **Depersonalizing:** *The figures don't seem to support that* (not *you are wrong*).

> **Atenção:** Diplomacy is register-dependent. In an internal memo to your own team, excessive softening sounds evasive. In a letter to a client or regulator, directness sounds rude. Judge the audience.

## 7. Typical errors made by Portuguese speakers

| Erro | Correção | Comentário |
|---|---|---|
| ~~He must forgot.~~ | He must have forgotten. | Dedução no passado: *must have + particípio*. |
| ~~She had to be tired.~~ | She must have been tired. | *Had to* é obrigação, não dedução. |
| ~~It can be that…~~ | It may be that… / It could be that… | *Can* não expressa probabilidade pontual. |
| ~~You don't must…~~ | You mustn't… / You don't have to… | *Must* é modal; sem *do*. |
| ~~That mustn't be right.~~ (dedução negativa) | That can't be right. | Dedução negativa usa *can't / couldn't*; *mustn't* é proibição. |
| ~~We would like to inform you that you are wrong.~~ | I'm afraid there seems to be a misunderstanding. | Polidez formal não é só uma frase formulaica. |
| ~~I need that you send me…~~ | I need you to send me… / Could you send me…? | Estrutura *need + object + to*. |

## Reading: critical text

Read this email from a supplier and decode the diplomatic language. What is the writer really saying, and how would a Brazilian colleague have phrased it?

> Dear Ms. Alencar,
>
> Thank you for your email of March 12. I was wondering whether we might revisit the delivery schedule we discussed last month. As you may be aware, there have been one or two disruptions in our supply chain, and it may well prove difficult to meet the original date of April 30. I should stress that we are bound to deliver the full order, and the quality of the components is not in question; it is simply that the timing might need to be a little more flexible than we had hoped. I appreciate that this is not ideal from your perspective, and I would not raise the matter were it avoidable. Would it be possible to arrange a short call this week to explore options? We could hardly expect you to absorb the full impact, and I am confident that a solution acceptable to both parties can be found.
>
> Kind regards,
> James Whitfield

Questions: How many hedging devices can you identify? What does "one or two disruptions" probably mean? Is the writer asking for permission or informing you of a decision?

## Na prática

Modality and hedging are the daily grammar of an accountant's correspondence. When you point out a problem to a client or an auditor, the epistemic scale lets you commit exactly as far as the evidence allows: {{en:The discrepancy may well stem from a timing difference, but we cannot rule out a duplicate entry.}} Diplomatic softening keeps a difficult message workable: {{en:I'm not sure the current provision is quite adequate; it might be worth revisiting the estimate.}} *Be to + infinitive* states formal arrangements (*The audit is to begin on 3 June*). Precision here protects you professionally: you are never caught overstating a conclusion.

## Resumo

- Modals have deontic (obligation, permission) and epistemic (probability) meanings; epistemic past uses *modal + have + past participle* (*must have forgotten*, not *had to forget*).
- The C1 certainty scale runs from *is bound to / is sure to* through *may well / could well* down to *could hardly / there is little chance*.
- *Be to + infinitive* expresses formal arrangements, instructions and destiny; *was to have done* marks unrealized plans.
- Hedging (*appears to, tends to, arguably, to some extent, it could be argued*) protects claims in academic and professional writing.
- Diplomatic English softens through past-tense distancing, downtoners, tentative questions and depersonalized statements; decode "I'm not sure that's quite right" as polite disagreement.
