# Participle clauses, reduced relatives and formal style

This lesson deals with the machinery of compact, formal English: participle clauses, reduced relative clauses, nominalization and sentence variety. These are the features that make a text read as "academic" or "professional" rather than conversational, and they are explicitly rewarded in CAE Writing (Part 1 essay) and IELTS Task 2 under *Grammatical Range and Accuracy*. They are also the source of some of the most common C1 errors, especially dangling participles.

> **Glossário de apoio:** *participle clause* = oração reduzida de particípio/gerúndio; *reduced relative* = oração relativa reduzida; *nominalization* = nominalização (transformar verbo ou adjetivo em substantivo); *dangling modifier* = modificador "solto", sem sujeito claro.

## 1. Participle clauses: the three forms

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| -ing (present participle) | action at the same time, or reason | {{en:Realizing the error, the accountant recalculated the depreciation.}} |
| -ed / irregular (past participle) | passive meaning | {{en:Audited by an independent firm, the accounts were accepted without question.}} |
| having + past participle (perfect) | earlier action | {{en:Having reviewed the contract, the lawyer recommended two amendments.}} |

The implied subject of the participle must be the **subject of the main clause**. That single rule prevents the "dangling participle":

- Wrong: ~~Walking into the office, the report was on the desk.~~ (the report was not walking)
- Right: *Walking into the office, she saw the report on the desk.*

Participle clauses can express:

- **Time:** *Having signed the deal, they celebrated.* (= after they had signed)
- **Reason:** *Being a cautious investor, he avoided the fund.* (= because he is)
- **Result:** *The firm lost its license, leaving 200 employees without work.*
- **Condition:** *Used correctly, the software saves hours.* (= if it is used)
- **Concession** (with *while / although*): *While acknowledging the risks, the board approved the plan.*

With conjunctions: *after, before, when, while, since, once, if, unless, although, as if*: {{en:Once approved, the budget cannot be altered without board consent.}}

Negative: *Not having received a reply, we sent a reminder.* Passive perfect: *Having been warned twice, he was dismissed.*

## 2. Reduced relative clauses

A defining relative clause with *who/which/that + be* (or an active verb) can often be reduced to a participle.

- *The clients who are waiting in reception* → {{en:The clients waiting in reception have been here for an hour.}}
- *The documents that were submitted late* → {{en:The documents submitted late will not be considered.}}
- *The firm that audits our accounts* → *The firm auditing our accounts* (active verb → -ing)
- *Anyone who wishes to apply* → *Anyone wishing to apply should contact HR.*
- With *to*-infinitive: *She was the first person who noticed* → *She was the first person to notice.* / *the only candidate to pass*

Reduction is **not** possible when the relative pronoun is the object (*the report which I wrote* cannot become ~~the report writing~~), or when the clause is non-defining with a stative verb (*my brother, who knows everything about tax*).

## 3. Nominalization

Nominalization turns verbs and adjectives into nouns so that a whole clause becomes a noun phrase. It is the engine of formal style.

| Verbal (informal) | Nominalized (formal) |
|---|---|
| The company failed to pay, so it was fined. | {{en:The company's failure to pay resulted in a fine.}} |
| Costs rose sharply, which worried investors. | {{en:The sharp rise in costs caused concern among investors.}} |
| We decided to postpone the launch because the market was unstable. | *The decision to postpone the launch was prompted by market instability.* |
| They analyzed the data carefully. | *A careful analysis of the data was carried out.* |

Advantages: density, objectivity (no human agent needed), easier linking between sentences (*This decision…*). Disadvantages: overuse makes prose heavy and vague ("the implementation of the facilitation of the optimization…"). Good C1 writers nominalize **the given information** at the start of a sentence and keep the new information in a verb or at the end.

Common nominalization patterns: -tion / -sion (*reduce → reduction*), -ment (*assess → assessment*), -ance / -ence (*comply → compliance*), -ity (*liable → liability*), -ness (*effective → effectiveness*), zero change (*increase, forecast, audit*).

## 4. Other markers of formal and academic style

- **Passive and impersonal structures:** *It is widely acknowledged that…*, *It has been suggested that…*, *Measures were introduced…*
- **Abstract subjects:** *This approach allows…*, *The findings indicate…*
- **Formal vocabulary:** *commence* (start), *terminate* (end), *ascertain* (find out), *subsequently* (then), *prior to* (before).
- **Avoidance of phrasal verbs and contractions:** *look into* → *investigate*; *put off* → *postpone*.
- **Hedging** (Lesson 4): *appears to, tends to, to some extent*.

> **Dica:** Formal is not the same as long. The best professional English is formal **and** concise. If a nominalization adds no information, cut it: *conduct an investigation into* → *investigate*.

## 5. Sentence variety

Examiners notice when every sentence follows the same pattern. Mix these:

1. Short simple sentence for impact: *The forecast was wrong.*
2. Participle opening: *Having underestimated demand, the firm ran out of stock.*
3. Fronted adverbial: *In the second quarter, margins recovered.*
4. Cleft or inversion (Lesson 1): *What saved the company was its cash reserve.*
5. Complex sentence with subordinate clause: *Although the forecast was wrong, the response was swift.*
6. Appositive noun phrase: *The CFO, a former auditor, insisted on monthly reviews.*
7. Semicolon linking two balanced ideas: *Revenue rose; costs rose faster.*

A useful discipline: in each paragraph, vary the **length** (one sentence under 8 words, one over 25) and the **opening** (never start three consecutive sentences with the subject).

## 6. Typical errors made by Portuguese speakers

| Erro | Correção | Comentário |
|---|---|---|
| ~~Being late, the meeting started without him.~~ | As he was late, the meeting started without him. | Dangling: a reunião não estava atrasada. |
| ~~After analyzed the data, we…~~ | After analyzing the data, we… | Depois de preposição/conjunção, -ing. |
| ~~The people which are working here~~ | The people working here | Reduz; e *which* não é para pessoas. |
| ~~Having finish the report~~ | Having finished the report | Particípio passado. |
| ~~The increasing of the costs~~ | The increase in costs | Nominalização pede o substantivo, não -ing + of. |
| ~~For to reduce the costs~~ | To reduce costs / In order to reduce costs | Calque de "para reduzir". |
| ~~The analyze of the data~~ | The analysis of the data | Verbo *analyze*, substantivo *analysis*. |

## Reading: critical text

The extract below comes from the summary section of a consultancy report. Note how participle clauses and nominalizations create density; then judge whether the text is clear or merely formal.

> Having reviewed the group's procurement processes over a six-month period, the review team identified three principal weaknesses. First, the absence of a centralized supplier database has resulted in duplicated contracts, several of which were negotiated at materially different prices. Second, approval thresholds, last revised in 2016, no longer reflect current transaction volumes, leaving a substantial proportion of spend subject to inadequate oversight. Third, the reliance on manual reconciliation increases the likelihood of error and delays the detection of irregularities. Taken together, these weaknesses expose the group to both financial and reputational risk. The implementation of the recommendations set out in Section 4, estimated to require an investment of 1.2 million over eighteen months, would address all three weaknesses and generate recurring savings exceeding that figure within three years. Failure to act, by contrast, would perpetuate inefficiencies already costing the group an estimated 800,000 annually.

Questions: Rewrite the final two sentences in plain, verbal style. Which version would you send to a board? Which to an operations manager?

## Na prática

The compact grammar of this lesson is what turns an accountant's notes into a report a board can read. Participle clauses condense procedure and result: {{en:Having reconciled the bank statements, we identified three unrecorded payments.}} Nominalization gives the findings an objective tone: {{en:The failure to accrue the bonus resulted in an understatement of liabilities.}} Reduced relative clauses tighten tables and notes (*the invoices issued in March*). Use them when the reader needs density and authority; switch back to full clauses in e-mails and meetings, where the same compression sounds cold. Always check the subject of every participle before sending.

## Resumo

- Participle clauses (-ing, -ed, having + -ed) replace adverbial clauses of time, reason, result and condition; their implied subject must match the main-clause subject, or the participle "dangles".
- Defining relative clauses with *who/which/that* can be reduced to -ing (active) or -ed (passive) participles, or to a *to*-infinitive after ordinals and superlatives.
- Nominalization (*fail → failure, comply → compliance*) packs clauses into noun phrases, creating formal, objective prose; use it for given information and cut it when it adds nothing.
- Formal style also relies on passive/impersonal structures, abstract subjects and Latinate vocabulary instead of phrasal verbs.
- Vary sentence length and openings deliberately; a paragraph in which every sentence starts with its subject reads as flat.
