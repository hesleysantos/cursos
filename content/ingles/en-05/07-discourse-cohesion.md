# Discourse and cohesion at C1

In this lesson you move from the sentence to the text. You will learn how paragraphs are built in English, how information flows from one sentence to the next (thematic progression), how to refer back to ideas economically (*the former / the latter, such, this + noun*), how ellipsis and substitution avoid repetition, and how to signpost a presentation so that listeners never get lost. In CAE and IELTS, these skills are assessed under *Organization* and *Coherence and Cohesion*, and they are what make a text read as written by a competent professional rather than a competent learner.

> **Glossário de apoio:** *cohesion* = coesão (ligações gramaticais e lexicais entre frases); *coherence* = coerência (lógica das ideias); *theme / rheme* = tema (informação dada, início da frase) / rema (informação nova, final da frase); *referencing* = referência (retomada de algo já mencionado); *ellipsis* = elipse (omissão); *substitution* = substituição (*one, do so, so, not*); *signposting* = sinalização verbal da estrutura.

## 1. The English paragraph

English academic and professional writing uses a **deductive** paragraph: the main idea comes first, support follows, and an optional closing sentence links forward. Portuguese writers often build toward the point; English readers expect it up front.

Structure:

1. **Topic sentence** – states the claim: {{en:Remote auditing has reduced costs but weakened the quality of evidence.}}
2. **Development** – explanation, example, data, contrast: *Travel expenses fell by 60%. However, auditors report difficulty in verifying physical inventory…*
3. **Closing / link** – consequence or bridge: *This trade-off is the subject of the next section.*

Each paragraph should have **one** controlling idea. If you can't summarize it in a single sentence, split it. A 250-word essay normally has four paragraphs; a report has titled sections with two or three paragraphs each.

## 2. Thematic progression: how information flows

Each sentence has a **theme** (what it is about, usually the subject) and a **rheme** (what is said about it, the new information). Good texts manage the flow between them in three patterns.

**Constant theme** (same subject repeated, good for descriptions):
*The new system records every transaction automatically. It flags duplicates. It also generates monthly summaries.*

**Linear progression** (the rheme of one sentence becomes the theme of the next, good for argument):
{{en:The audit revealed a shortfall of two million. This shortfall was traced to a single supplier. The supplier, it emerged, had been overcharging for years.}}

**Split rheme** (one sentence introduces two or more items, each developed in turn):
*The proposal has two weaknesses: cost and timing. The cost… The timing…*

The practical rule: **start sentences with known information and end with new information**. This is why the passive, fronting and nominalization (Lessons 1 and 3) exist: they let you reorder a sentence so that the given element comes first.

- Weak flow: *A new procedure was introduced in March. The finance team designed the procedure.*
- Strong flow: *A new procedure was introduced in March. It was designed by the finance team, who…*

## 3. Referencing devices

Repetition of full noun phrases is heavy; pronouns alone can be ambiguous. C1 writers use a middle layer.

| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **the former / the latter** (two items only) | {{en:Both audits and reviews provide assurance, but the former is far more rigorous than the latter.}} |
| **this / these + summarizing noun** | *Costs rose while revenue fell. **This combination** forced the company to borrow.* (also *this approach, this trend, this measure, this view, these factors, such a step*) |
| **such** | *The firm reported record losses. **Such results** were not unexpected.* |
| **the above / the following / as follows** | *The following criteria were applied…* |
| **respectively** | *Revenue and costs rose by 5% and 9% respectively.* |
| **the one… the other** | *There are two options: the one is cheap but slow, the other fast but costly.* |
| **the same / likewise / similarly** | *Branch A exceeded its target; Branch B did likewise.* |
| **hereafter / hereinafter** (legal) | *Alencar & Partners (hereinafter "the Firm")…* |

> **Dica:** *This + summarizing noun* is the single most useful cohesive device in formal English. Instead of a bare *This*, which readers must decode, write *This shortfall / This decision / This approach*. It tells the reader how to interpret the previous sentence.

## 4. Ellipsis and substitution

Mature writing omits what is recoverable from context.

**Ellipsis** (leaving words out):

- *The first quarter was strong; the second [quarter was] weak.*
- *We expected a loss but [we] made a profit.*
- Infinitive ellipsis: *You can leave early if you want to [leave early].*
- After auxiliaries: *She hasn't signed yet, but she will [sign].*

**Substitution** (replacing with a pro-form):

- *one / ones*: {{en:The old procedure was slow; the new one is almost instantaneous.}}
- *do so / do it / do that*: *The board asked management to revise the forecast, and they did so within a week.*
- *so / not* after verbs of thinking: *Will the client accept? I hope so / I'm afraid not / I suspect not.*
- *the same*: *He ordered coffee and I did the same.*
- *such*: *Any such claim must be submitted in writing.*

## 5. Linking at C1: beyond *however* and *moreover*

You know the basic discourse markers. At C1, variety and precision matter.

| Function | C1 options |
|---|---|
| Adding | *furthermore, in addition to this, not only… but also, what is more, equally* |
| Contrasting | *nevertheless, nonetheless, by contrast, conversely, whereas, albeit, that said, even so* |
| Conceding | *admittedly, granted, while it is true that, notwithstanding* |
| Cause/result | *consequently, hence, thereby, as a result of which, thus* |
| Exemplifying | *notably, in particular, a case in point is, to illustrate* |
| Reformulating | *in other words, that is to say, to put it another way* |
| Sequencing | *initially, subsequently, in due course, ultimately* |
| Summarizing | *in short, on balance, all things considered, taken together* |

{{en:The scheme is costly; nonetheless, on balance, the benefits outweigh the drawbacks.}}

Avoid starting every sentence with a linker. Native-like cohesion comes mostly from referencing and lexical chains (synonyms, hyponyms: *the company → the firm → the retailer → it*), not from connectors.

## 6. Signposting in presentations

Spoken discourse needs explicit signposts because listeners cannot re-read.

- **Opening:** *Good morning. My name is… and today I'll be looking at three things: first… second… and finally…*
- **Transitions:** {{en:That brings me to my second point, which is cash flow.}} / *Having looked at costs, let's turn to revenue.* / *Moving on…*
- **Referring to visuals:** *If you look at the chart on the left, you'll notice…* / *As this slide shows…*
- **Emphasizing:** *The key point here is…* / *I cannot stress this enough:…*
- **Digressing and returning:** *Just as an aside… Anyway, to get back to the main issue…*
- **Summarizing:** *So, to sum up: three points…* / *Let me recap briefly.*
- **Closing and Q&A:** {{en:Thank you for your attention. I'd be happy to take any questions.}}

## 7. Typical errors made by Portuguese speakers

| Erro | Correção | Comentário |
|---|---|---|
| Parágrafo que só revela a ideia no fim | Topic sentence primeiro | Leitor inglês espera a ideia principal no início. |
| ~~This~~ sozinho, repetido | This measure / This decision | *This* nu é ambíguo; some um substantivo. |
| ~~The first… the latter~~ | The former… the latter | Par fixo. |
| ~~In the other hand~~ | On the other hand | Preposição fixa. |
| ~~Despite of~~ | Despite / In spite of | *Despite* não leva *of*. |
| ~~For a side… for other side~~ | On the one hand… on the other hand | Calque de "por um lado". |
| ~~Like I said before~~ (escrito formal) | As mentioned above / As stated earlier | *Like* é informal. |
| ~~He made the audit and we made the same.~~ | He conducted the audit and we did the same. | *Make* vs *do*; colocação. |

## Reading: critical text

Read the opening of a report section and trace the cohesion: underline every referencing device and identify the thematic progression.

> The department's two main processes, invoicing and collection, were reviewed in parallel. The former is largely automated and generated few exceptions; the latter, by contrast, relies on manual follow-up and accounts for most of the delays identified. These delays have two causes. The first is structural: credit terms vary by client, and no single report consolidates them. The second is behavioral: staff tend to prioritize new invoices over overdue ones, and such prioritization, while understandable, allows older debts to age further. This pattern was evident in all three regional offices, albeit to differing degrees. Addressing it would require not only a consolidated aging report but also a change in incentives. The former could be delivered within a quarter; the latter would take longer, as it involves renegotiating performance targets. Taken together, these measures would, in our estimate, reduce average collection time by roughly a third.

Questions: How many times does the writer use *the former / the latter*, and does the second use work? Find an example of linear progression. Which *this + noun* phrase summarizes the most material?

## Na prática

Cohesion is what makes a management letter or a reconciliation report readable at speed. Open each paragraph with the finding, then develop it: given information first, new information last. Reference devices save words and prevent ambiguity: {{en:We reviewed both the payroll ledger and the bank file; the latter contained two unexplained transfers.}} In a presentation to a client's finance team, signposting keeps everyone oriented: {{en:That brings me to the second issue: the unreconciled supplier balances.}} Before sending a report, read only the first sentence of each paragraph; if they form a coherent summary, the structure works.

## Resumo

- English paragraphs are deductive: topic sentence first, development, optional link; one controlling idea per paragraph.
- Manage information flow by starting with given information and ending with new information; linear progression (rheme → theme) drives argument.
- Reference economically with *the former / the latter, this + summarizing noun, such, respectively*, and avoid bare *this*.
- Use ellipsis and substitution (*one, do so, so / not, the same*) to avoid repetition, and build cohesion mainly through lexical chains rather than connectors.
- In presentations, signpost explicitly: outline, transitions (*that brings me to…*), emphasis, recap and Q&A invitation.
