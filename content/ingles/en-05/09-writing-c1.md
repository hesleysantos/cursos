# Writing at C1: essay, report, proposal, review, and critical reading

This final lesson of the module brings together everything you have studied and applies it to the four written genres assessed at C1: the discursive essay, the report, the proposal and the review. You will learn the structure, register and assessment criteria for each, read an opinion article and a research summary critically, and practice the exam strategies that distinguish a band 7.5 or a CAE grade A from a merely adequate performance.

> **Glossário de apoio:** *task achievement / content* = cumprimento da tarefa; *register* = registro; *target reader* = leitor-alvo; *stance* = posicionamento do autor; *inference* = inferência (o que o texto implica sem dizer); *rubric* = critérios de avaliação.

## 1. How C1 writing is assessed

Both Cambridge and IELTS use four criteria of roughly equal weight.

| Criterion | What examiners look for |
|---|---|
| **Content / Task achievement** | All points of the task addressed; the target reader is fully informed; a clear position (essay) or recommendation (report, proposal). |
| **Communicative achievement / Register** | Genre conventions followed; tone consistent and appropriate to the reader; ideas communicated with effect. |
| **Organization / Coherence and cohesion** | Logical paragraphs; varied cohesive devices (Lesson 7); headings where appropriate. |
| **Language / Lexical and grammatical range and accuracy** | Wide range of structures (Lessons 1–4) and less common vocabulary (Lessons 5–6) used naturally; errors rare and non-impeding. |

A common C1 failure is **imbalance**: perfect grammar with thin content, or rich ideas with no paragraph structure. Plan for all four.

## 2. The discursive essay (CAE Part 1, IELTS Task 2)

**Structure (220–260 words CAE; 250+ IELTS):**

1. Introduction: paraphrase the issue, state your position or the scope. {{en:Whether governments should subsidize failing industries is a question that divides economists and voters alike.}}
2. Body paragraph 1: first point, explanation, example, evaluation.
3. Body paragraph 2: second point (or counter-argument + rebuttal).
4. Conclusion: weigh the points, restate your position with a nuance. {{en:On balance, while short-term support may be justified, open-ended subsidies ultimately entrench inefficiency.}}

**Register:** formal-neutral. No contractions, no rhetorical overkill, hedged claims (Lesson 4). Use *This + noun* linking and one or two emphatic devices (Lesson 1) at key moments.

**Useful frames:** *It is often argued that… / There is a strong case for… / Admittedly, …; nevertheless, … / A further consideration is… / Were this policy adopted, … / What this overlooks is…*

## 3. The report

A report is written **for** a specific reader (manager, committee, board) and **about** a situation, with findings and recommendations.

- **Title and headings**: *Introduction / Current situation / Findings / Recommendations* (or question-specific headings). Headings are expected; they organize the reader's attention.
- **Introduction**: purpose and scope: {{en:The aim of this report is to evaluate the current expenses procedure and to recommend improvements.}}
- **Findings**: factual, impersonal, past or present tense; data where possible; passive and nominalization (Lesson 3) are natural here.
- **Recommendations**: modal verbs and formal imperatives: *It is recommended that the policy be revised. / The department should consider… / Management may wish to…*
- **Register**: formal, objective. Avoid *I think*; use *The evidence suggests*.

Typical reader-oriented sentence: *It is clear from the feedback that staff would welcome clearer guidelines.*

## 4. The proposal

A proposal looks **forward**: it argues for a course of action and asks for approval or funding. Structure resembles the report, but the balance shifts to persuasion.

- *Introduction (purpose) / Background or Current problems / Proposal / Benefits / Conclusion*.
- Persuasive but measured: {{en:Implementing a shared-services model would not only cut costs by an estimated 15% but also free senior staff for higher-value work.}}
- Anticipate objections: *Although the initial outlay is significant, the investment would be recouped within two years.*
- End with a clear ask: *I would therefore urge the committee to approve the pilot scheme.*

## 5. The review

A review evaluates something (a book, course, software, restaurant) for a general or specialist readership, often in a magazine or on a website.

- Engaging opening; description; evaluation of strengths and weaknesses; recommendation to the reader.
- Register: semi-formal to informal, personal voice allowed (*I found…*), vivid adjectives, idioms permitted in moderation (Lesson 5).
- Balanced judgment is rewarded: *The interface is intuitive; the reporting module, by contrast, is frustratingly limited.*
- {{en:Despite its flaws, this is the most useful accounting app I have tested this year, and at that price it is hard to fault.}}

## 6. Exam strategies

1. **Analyze the task** (2 min): underline every content point and the target reader. Missing one content point caps your score.
2. **Plan** (5 min): one line per paragraph, with the key idea and one piece of evidence. Decide your position before writing.
3. **Write** (30 min): topic sentence first in each paragraph; vary sentence openings; insert one inversion or cleft deliberately; use hedging for claims.
4. **Check** (3–5 min): the errors Brazilians make under pressure — articles (*the* before generic plurals), subject-verb agreement, *despite of*, *in the other hand*, comma splices, *actually / eventually*, missing *-s* in third person.
5. **Word count**: slightly over is fine; under is penalized. Do not pad with repetition.
6. **IELTS-specific**: answer the exact question asked (*to what extent, discuss both views, advantages outweigh disadvantages*). Give a clear overall position in the introduction and conclusion.
7. **CAE-specific**: Part 1 essay requires you to discuss two of three given points and say which is more important. In Part 2, choose the genre you know best; conventions (headings for reports, title for reviews) count toward communicative achievement.

## 7. Critical reading: an opinion article

Read the extract and answer the inference questions that follow.

> It has become fashionable to describe the four-day week as inevitable, as though productivity were a law of nature rather than a management outcome. Proponents point to pilot schemes in which output held steady while hours fell. What they rarely mention is that these pilots were voluntary, short, and heavily self-selected: the firms that joined were precisely those already confident of the result. Nor is it clear that "output" was measured in any way an accountant would recognize. Were the four-day week genuinely cost-free, one would expect profit-maximizing companies to have adopted it decades ago; that they have not suggests either that managers are irrational, which is possible, or that the costs are real but diffuse, which is more likely. None of this means the idea is wrong. It means the evidence is thinner than the enthusiasm.

Inference questions: (a) What is the author's stance: hostile, skeptical or neutral? Which words signal it? (b) What does the phrase "in any way an accountant would recognize" imply about the pilots' measurement? (c) The author offers two explanations for non-adoption. Which does the author favor, and how do you know? (d) Is the final sentence a concession or a conclusion?

Suggested answers: the stance is **skeptical, not hostile**: *fashionable*, *as though*, *rarely mention*, *thinner than the enthusiasm* all undercut the proponents, but *None of this means the idea is wrong* explicitly refuses outright opposition. The accountant phrase implies that output was measured loosely or subjectively. The author favors the "real but diffuse costs" explanation, signaled by *more likely* versus *possible*. The final sentence is a conclusion framed as a concession.

## 8. Critical reading: a research summary

> We analyzed 1,300 small and medium-sized enterprises over five years to test whether digital invoicing reduces payment delays. Firms adopting e-invoicing saw average collection periods fall by 11 days relative to non-adopters. However, adopters were larger and more profitable before adoption, and when we control for these characteristics the effect falls to 4 days and is only marginally significant. We therefore caution against attributing the full observed improvement to the technology itself. The results are consistent with, though do not establish, a modest causal effect.

Questions: (a) What is the headline finding, and what is the finding after controls? (b) Why do the authors "caution"? (c) What does "consistent with, though do not establish" mean in plain English? (d) How would a journalist likely misreport this study?

## 9. Typical errors made by Portuguese speakers in writing tasks

| Erro | Correção | Comentário |
|---|---|---|
| Introdução longa, posição só no fim | Posição na introdução | O leitor inglês quer a tese cedo. |
| ~~In my opinion I think that…~~ | In my view, … / I believe that… | Redundância. |
| ~~The people think…~~ (genérico) | People think… | Sem artigo em plural genérico. |
| Relatório sem títulos | *Introduction / Findings / Recommendations* | Convenção do gênero. |
| ~~I recommend to implement~~ | I recommend implementing / that the firm implement | Padrão do verbo. |
| Vírgula ligando duas orações (comma splice) | Ponto, ponto e vírgula ou conector | Erro grave em C1/C2. |
| ~~According to me~~ | In my opinion / As I see it | *According to* é para terceiros. |
| Misturar *you* informal em relatório | Impessoal / *staff, management, the reader* | Registro. |

## Na prática

The four genres of this lesson map directly onto an accountant's output: the report is the audit or review report, the proposal is the engagement letter or fee proposal, the essay trains the argued memo and the review sharpens evaluative judgment. Use the report structure for findings and recommendations: {{en:It is recommended that the company adopt a monthly reconciliation routine.}} Use the proposal structure when you want approval: {{en:This proposal outlines a three-phase migration to the new tax regime and its expected savings.}} Plan every document, write with deliberate emphasis and run the Brazilian-error checklist before the client sees it.

## Resumo

- C1 writing is assessed on content, communicative achievement (register and genre), organization and language range; plan for all four, not just grammar.
- Essay: position early, two developed body paragraphs, nuanced conclusion. Report: headings, impersonal findings, recommendations with *should / it is recommended that*. Proposal: forward-looking persuasion with a clear ask. Review: personal voice, balanced evaluation, recommendation.
- Analyze the task, plan, write with varied openings and deliberate emphasis, then check the classic Brazilian errors (articles, agreement, *despite of*, comma splices).
- Critical reading means identifying stance from word choice (*fashionable, as though, rarely*), distinguishing hedged from asserted claims, and noticing what the author concedes.
- Research summaries must be read for the finding **after** controls and for phrases such as *consistent with, though do not establish*, which signal weak causal claims.
